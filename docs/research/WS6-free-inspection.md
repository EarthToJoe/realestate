# Free inspections help sellers, not buyers

**The free upfront inspection should not launch as proposed, because its central promise fails: buyers in the Hudson Valley will keep hiring their own inspector, so the seller does not avoid "a second one" and the brokerage pays for a report nobody else relies on.** That hits the kill criterion of assumption A9 ("buyers do not skip their own inspection anyway, so it is a pure cost") for the proposal as stated; the criterion's other limb is dodged, because liability *can* be contained if a licensed, insured inspector works under contract for the seller. Three further findings shape the answer. First, under New York law the friend must hold a home inspector licence before anyone pays him, and a brokerage that "permits or authorizes" an unlicensed inspection commits a misdemeanor ([RPL §§444-d, 444-o, DOS compilation March 2026](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)) [PRIMARY]; a free or discounted "favour" from him to a brokerage that controls future buyer referrals is the arrangement both New York's inspector code and the federal anti-kickback law (RESPA) forbid. Second, Joe's reservation is not just a sales worry, it is the law: a report turns the seller's "unknown" into "known", the Property Condition Disclosure Statement (PCDS) must then be answered from that knowledge, any radon test must be attached to it, and for pre-1978 houses any report touching lead paint must go to buyers under federal law, with the brokerage charged to make sure it does. Third, a genuinely "comprehensive" upstate package is several separately licensed services and costs about **$550–$1,025 for a village house and $1,100–$2,250 for a rural house with well and septic (October 2026)**, paid whether or not the house sells, with no study showing it sells homes faster or for more. My position: drop the proposal as stated; run a narrowed, optional, seller-engaged version as a capped pilot (Design 2 below), never market it to buyers, and fall back to an inspection credit and a disclosed list of licensed inspectors if the pilot shows no benefit. The narrowed version is also one of the best demo moments in the product, because every legal checkpoint is visible.

## Buyers will still hire their own inspector

The father-in-law's pitch rests on the buyer feeling no need to pay for an inspection once the seller has one. Every strand of evidence found points the other way, and the Hudson Valley's own deal sequence is the first reason. Westchester and the Hudson Valley counties that share its "downstate" customs (WS1 notes) run inspection *before* contract: after an offer is accepted the brokers circulate a memorandum, and the seller's attorney drafts the contract "only after engineering inspections are complete" ([Rand Realty, via WS1 notes](https://jeselle.randrealty.com/?p=79)) [SECONDARY]; the State Bar's consumer guide puts the inspection before the binder and contract ([NYSBA](https://nysba.org/5-steps-for-buying-a-home-in-new-york-state/)) [SECONDARY]. The standard contract then has the buyer acknowledge that it is "fully aware of the physical condition and state of repair of the premises" on the strength of its own inspection and take the house "as is" ([Calvente v. Levy, quoting the clause](https://www.nycourts.gov/REPORTER/3dseries/2006/2006_26163.htm)) [PRIMARY, via search snippet]. A New York buyer therefore has no inspection contingency to "waive"; skipping means hiring nobody before signing away the right to complain. The national figure people quote, that **20% of buyers waived the inspection contingency in September 2026** ([NAR Confidence Index, released 13 Oct 2026](https://www.nar.realtor/sites/default/files/2026-10/2026-10-realtors-confidence-index-10-13-2026_1.pdf)) [PRIMARY], measures bidding-war tactics in states where the inspection follows the contract, not trust in a seller's report: waivers rose in the competitive markets of 2020 ([Redfin, 2020](https://www.redfin.com/news/waiving-inspection-contingency-wins-bidding-wars/)) [SECONDARY, pre-2024], and the New York bill introduced in 2025 targeted sellers who make acceptance of an offer conditional on a waiver ([S5772](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/S5772)) [PRIMARY].

Lenders do not change this. No lender found requires a home inspection, and none accepts a seller's report in place of the appraisal it does require: FHA hands every buyer a notice that "A home inspection will only occur if you arrange for one" and that "Appraisals are NOT Home Inspections!" ([HUD 92564-CN](https://www.hud.gov/sites/dfiles/OCHCO/documents/92564-CN.pdf)) [PRIMARY]. VA loans require a separate termite report in 18 New York counties, including Columbia, Dutchess, Greene, Orange, Putnam, Rockland, Sullivan, Ulster and Westchester ([Veterans United, citing VA Circular 26-22-11, updated 25 Sept 2026](https://www.veteransunited.com/valoans/va-termite-pest-inspections/)) [SECONDARY], and USDA treats well and septic inspections as valid for only 120 days before closing ([USDA training slides](https://rd.usda.gov/files/RD-SFH-SiteRequirements1.pdf)) [SECONDARY, search snippet; page blocked], so pre-listing tests often have to be repeated for those buyers anyway.

Lawyers and the real estate trade tell buyers the same thing. The Long Island Board of REALTORS' guidance on the 2024 disclosure law asks whether the PCDS should take the place of an inspection and answers "Absolutely not" ([LIBOR PCDS FAQ, Rev. 4/10/2025](https://www.lirealtor.com/docs/default-source/default-document-library/PCDS-FAQ.pdf)) [SECONDARY]; the leading New York commentator on the statute warns that "Buyers lose many cases" when they fail to check condition ([NYSBA, Holtzschue, 2024](https://nysba.org/breaking-news-new-rules-on-property-condition-disclosure-and-flood-risk-go-into-effect-today/)) [SECONDARY]; and the statutory form itself tells the buyer "THE BUYER IS ENCOURAGED TO OBTAIN HIS OR HER OWN INDEPENDENT PROFESSIONAL INSPECTIONS" ([RPL §462](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/462)) [PRIMARY]. Most telling is the inspection industry's own paperwork. InterNACHI markets its seller-inspection programme to agents by saying the report "may persuade a buyer to waive his own inspection contingency" ([InterNACHI agent page](https://www.nachi.org/moveincertified/agents)) [CLAIMED], yet its published pre-listing contract tells the seller the report is "for your use only", that third parties rely "at their own risk", and that "Our report is not a substitute for a buyer's home inspection once the property is under contract" ([InterNACHI Pre-Listing Inspection Agreement, rev. Nov 2017](https://nachi.org/pre-listing-agreement.htm)) [PRIMARY].

Two countries have run the experiment the pitch assumes. England required sellers to provide a Home Information Pack with an optional condition report; the government that scrapped it in 2010 cited Ipsos MORI research that "over 90% of people buying a house wanted information about its condition" but "fewer than 1 in 6 trusted this information when provided by the seller" ([gov.uk, 10 Sept 2010](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/hips-failed-because-no-one-trusted-them)) [PRIMARY]. Scotland went the other way: its mandatory Home Report includes a seller-commissioned survey whose author can be liable to the *buyer* under regulation ([Scottish Parliament Information Centre, 2019](https://spice-spotlight.scot/2019/05/22/home-reports-what-are-they-and-what-might-change/)) [PRIMARY], and there only **23% of buyers** commissioned a further survey or valuation, most of them lender valuations ([Scottish Government review research, 2014 survey, 928 responses](https://www.gov.scot/publications/research-inform-five-year-review-home-report/pages/8/)) [PRIMARY, via search snippet; page bot-blocked]. The lesson is that buyers rely on a seller's report only when the report's author answers to them. New York's statute does the opposite: "The duty of every home inspector shall be to the client" ([RPL §444-g(1)](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)) [PRIMARY]. Even in Seattle, where two-thirds of single-family sales over $700,000 in one 2023 sample shared a seller-procured report, local inspectors said buyer pre-offer inspections were still a third to half of their work, because some buyers do not trust seller reports ([Seattle realtor's blog, June 2023](https://www.myseattlehomesearch.com/blog/once-rare-now-common-seller-procured-home-inspections/)) [SECONDARY]; the only US estimate specific to pre-listing reports is an Indiana inspector's anecdote that buyers still inspect "about 80% of the time" ([ASHI Reporter, 2020](https://www.homeinspector.org/Resources/Pre-Listing-Inspection)) [SECONDARY].

New York may soon make the point statutory. The Right to Home Inspection Act (S8729-B / A9629-C, amended May 2026, still in the Judiciary committees with no floor vote) would give buyers a non-waivable right to their own licensed inspection within ten business days of a signed agreement, bar sellers and agents from accepting an offer from a buyer who says it will waive, and treat agent violations as deceptive practices under General Business Law §349 ([nysenate.gov S8729](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/S8729); [A9629](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/A9629)) [PRIMARY]. **So A9's kill criterion is hit for the proposal as pitched**: as a substitute for the buyer's inspection it is a pure cost. The brief's line that "the seller doesn't pay for a second one" also mixes up who pays: the buyer pays for the buyer's inspection. What survives is the seller-side benefit of knowing early, which is real but unmeasured, and that is what the narrowed design is built around.

## The friend must hold a licence and work for the seller

New York has required home inspectors to be licensed since the end of 2005 ([2007 NY Slip Op 50276(U)](https://www.nycourts.gov/reporter/3dseries/2007/2007_50276.htm)) [SECONDARY, via search snippet]. Nobody may "conduct or represent that he or she has the ability to conduct a home inspection for compensation" without an Article 12-B licence, unless they are a New York-licensed architect or engineer acting within scope or a code official ([RPL §444-d](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)) [PRIMARY]. The penalty reaches the brokerage, not just the inspector: anyone who "directly or indirectly employs, permits or authorizes an unlicensed person to engage in the business of home inspection" is guilty of a misdemeanor, fined up to $1,000 for a first conviction and $1,000–$5,000 after, each violation a separate offence ([RPL §444-o](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)) [PRIMARY], and the broker's own licence is then exposed to discipline for "untrustworthiness" ([RPL §441-c](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/441-C)) [PRIMARY]. A licence takes 140 hours of approved education including 40 hours of supervised field inspections (or 100 supervised inspections), a national exam, $250, and renewal every two years with continuing education ([RPL §§444-e, 444-f](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)) [PRIMARY]. Whether the friend holds one takes seconds to check: the State's open-data register of licensed home inspectors, refreshed on 22 October 2026, lists **2,220 unexpired licences statewide, 464 of them based in Westchester, Orange, Dutchess, Rockland, Ulster, Putnam, Columbia, Sullivan and Greene** ([data.ny.gov dataset r9fr-48vu, queried 2026-10-23](https://data.ny.gov/d/r9fr-48vu)) [PRIMARY]. The friend is a convenience, not a necessity, and the software can verify any inspector's licence automatically before a booking.

"Comprehensive" is not one licensed service. The statutory home inspection "shall not include an inspection for radon or pests" ([RPL §444-b(4)](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)) [PRIMARY]; the standards of practice excuse inspectors from evaluating wells, water quality and "on-site sewage disposal systems such as cesspools, septic tanks, drain fields" ([19 NYCRR §197-5.8(c)(12)](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)) [PRIMARY]; and inspections are "not technically exhaustive" and need not find mold, lead paint, buried oil tanks or code violations ([§197-5.16](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)) [PRIMARY]. Radon and water samples must be analysed by state-certified laboratories ([Public Health Law §502](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/PBH/502)) [PRIMARY], and practitioners report that a termite report on the standard form needs a state pesticide certification ([InterNACHI forum](https://forum.nachi.org/t/nys-wdo-termite-certification/94339)) [CLAIMED]. An upstate "everything" package is therefore an inspection plus radon, water, septic and termite services from several providers, each with its own contract.

Who hires the inspector decides who he works for and who may use the report. The "client" is whoever "engages or seeks to engage" him ([RPL §444-b(1)](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)) [PRIMARY]; his duty runs to that client alone, and the report "shall be deemed confidential and shall not be disclosed without the express consent of the client" ([RPL §444-g(1), (3)](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf); [19 NYCRR §197-4.3](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)) [PRIMARY]. If the brokerage is the client, the brokerage owns a confidential report about its own listing, which is the worst position for it (see the next section). If the seller is the client, the seller controls the report and the brokerage sees it only with the seller's consent. Buyers who later read it are not the inspector's clients. New York's highest court allows a non-client to sue a professional for a careless report only where the professional knew the specific party would rely on it; "The words 'known party or parties' ... mean what they say" ([Sykes v. RFD Third Ave. 1 Assocs., 15 N.Y.3d 370 (2010)](https://static.case.law/ny3d/15/html/0370-01.html); building on [Ossining, 73 N.Y.2d 417 (1989)](https://static.case.law/ny-2d/73/html/0417-01.html)) [PRIMARY]. A report written for a seller before any buyer exists sits comfortably outside that test, as a Washington appeals court held in 2024 when it dismissed a buyer's claims against a seller's pre-listing inspector ([Apgood v. Plautz, via Beresford Booth](https://beresfordlaw.com/pre-listing-inspection-reports-truths-and-myths/)) [SECONDARY]. But a programme built for buyers to rely on moves toward the facts of an Iowa case in which a relocation company commissioned the inspection, the buyer relied on it instead of hiring his own, and the court held the inspector owed him a duty because the report contemplated being shared ([Burbach v. Radon Analytical Lab., Iowa 2002, NAR summary](https://www.nar.realtor/legal-case-summaries/burbach-v-radon-analytical-lab-inc-purchasers-lawsuit-against-home-inspector-over-report-allowed-to)) [SECONDARY]. Hosting the report for buyers, summarising it in listing copy, or telling buyers they need not inspect all push the programme in that direction.

The conflict rules govern how the brokerage may pay. Before accepting the job the inspector must disclose to his client "all known or potential conflicts of interest", may not accept compensation from more than one interested party "unless the circumstances are fully disclosed to the client and agreed upon by all interested parties", may not "directly or indirectly compensate, in any way" a brokerage "for future referrals of inspections or for inclusion on a list of recommended inspectors", and may not take a job whose pay "is contingent upon the sale of the home" ([19 NYCRR §197-4.7(b), (c), (e), (i)](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)) [PRIMARY]. The statute backs this with licence discipline and fines of up to $1,000 per violation for accepting pay from more than one interested party without everyone's consent or failing to disclose a business interest ([RPL §444-h(1)(c), (e)](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)) [PRIMARY], and the Department of State has revoked an inspector's licence and ordered restitution for an undisclosed business relationship with another inspection firm ([DOS v. Karl, 2008](https://docs.dos.ny.gov/ooah/decisions/homeinspector/Karl_Kenneth.htm)) [PRIMARY]. The inspector also may not do repairs, for an extra fee, on systems he reported on before closing (components outside the standards of practice excepted) ([RPL §444-g(4)(a)](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)) [PRIMARY], so "the friend inspects, then fixes" is out. On the brokerage's side, New York has no rule against recommending an inspector (Massachusetts bars listing agents from recommending a specific inspector at all ([M.G.L. c. 112 §87YY½](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXVI/Chapter112/Section87YY%201~2)) [PRIMARY], a useful warning of how regulators view the conflict), and giving a seller a free service is a lawful incentive: "The payment of cash or offering an incentive to encourage a consumer to do business with a broker is permitted" ([DOS broker FAQ](https://dos.ny.gov/real-estate-broker-frequently-asked-questions)) [PRIMARY].

Federal law closes the door on the "favour". RESPA (the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act) treats both inspections and broker services as "settlement services", defined as any service "in connection with a prospective or actual settlement" ([12 CFR 1024.2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/1024.2)) [PRIMARY]; it bars any "thing of value" given for referrals, a term that covers discounts and services at special rates and needs no transfer of money, with an agreement inferable from "a practice, pattern or course of conduct" ([12 CFR 1024.14](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/1024.14)) [PRIMARY]; and it carries criminal fines up to $10,000 and civil liability of three times the charge ([12 U.S.C. 2607](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/12/2607)) [PRIMARY]. A friend who inspects the brokerage's listings free or cheap while the brokerage steers buyers his way fits that description; a brokerage that pays his full retail price for work actually done and promises nothing in return does not. The lawful structure is therefore narrow and specific: a licensed inspector, the seller signing as his client, a written disclosure (signed by seller, brokerage and inspector) that the brokerage pays a fixed retail fee and that the inspector knows the brokerage's principal, a fee owed whether or not the house sells, and nothing flowing back from the inspector to the brokerage.

## A report turns "unknown" into "known"

Joe's worry that not every seller will want to make every fix is correct, and New York law turns it from a sales problem into a disclosure trap. Since 20 March 2024 every seller of a one-to-four-family house must deliver a signed 56-question PCDS before the buyer signs a contract, with no $500 opt-out ([RPL §462](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/462); [NYSBA, Holtzschue](https://nysba.org/pcda-amended-500-seller-credit-deleted-and-additional-questions-added-to-pcds/)) [PRIMARY/SECONDARY]. The seller's protection is that "knowledge" means "only actual knowledge" and that the law does not require any inspection ([RPL §461(3)](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/461); [§462(3)](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/462)) [PRIMARY]. A pre-listing inspection deliberately removes that protection: every finding becomes something the seller actually knows, and the form asks about known defects system by system. Some answers require the report itself. In the current statutory form, Q24 asks "HAS A RADON TEST BEEN DONE? (IF YES, ATTACH A COPY OF THE REPORT)", and the petroleum (Q26), mold (Q27) and pest (Q31) testing questions likewise require attachments, while Q35 asks whether water quality or flow has been tested ([RPL §462(2)](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/462)) [PRIMARY]. (The research notes had used the old form's numbering; the Department of State's own PCDS link still redirects to the superseded June 2023 form with the abolished $500 credit, so the product must work from the statute's text ([dos.ny.gov link, checked 2026-10-23](https://dos.ny.gov/property-condition-disclosure-statement)) [PRIMARY].) A seller who learns something that makes an earlier PCDS materially inaccurate must deliver a revised one as soon as practicable ([LIBOR PCDS FAQ](https://www.lirealtor.com/docs/default-source/default-document-library/PCDS-FAQ.pdf)) [SECONDARY]. A seller who answers wilfully wrong is liable for the buyer's actual damages "in addition to any other existing equitable or statutory remedy" ([RPL §465](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/465)) [PRIMARY].

New York still follows caveat emptor ("buyer beware"), but its exceptions are exactly where a pre-listing report puts the seller. Mere silence is not fraud, yet a seller's knowing nondisclosure of a condition it created and peculiarly knows can justify undoing the sale ([Stambovsky v. Ackley, 1st Dept 1991](https://static.case.law/ad2d/169/html/0254-01.html)) [PRIMARY], and "When a seller makes a false representation in a Disclosure Statement, such a representation may be proof of active concealment" ([Simone v. Homecheck, 2d Dept 2007](https://static.case.law/ad3d/42/html/0518-01.html)) [PRIMARY]. An "as is" clause defeated the buyer's contract claim in Simone but not the fraud claim, and New York commentary is blunt that "Neither the 'as is' nor merger clauses will generally protect the seller from a claim of fraud" ([NYSBA, Holtzschue](https://nysba.org/pcda-amended-500-seller-credit-deleted-and-additional-questions-added-to-pcds/)) [SECONDARY]. The flip side is reassuring for honest sellers: where nothing suggests the seller or brokers thwarted the buyer's own checks and the contract carries an "as is" clause and a specific disclaimer, fraud claims fail, as the Second Department held in June 2026 in a case brought against a seller, a brokerage and its listing agent ([Serba v. Cook, 2026 NY Slip Op 03464, via Fox Horan summary](https://www.fhnylaw.com/caveat-emptor-in-an-as-is-world-fraud-in-the-purchase-and-sale-of-real-property)) [SECONDARY; court text bot-blocked]; see also [Masters v. Visual Bldg. Inspections, 2d Dept 1996](https://static.case.law/ad2d/227/html/0597-01.html) [PRIMARY].

Federal lead-paint law widens the trap for older housing. Before a buyer is bound, the seller of a pre-1978 house must disclose known lead-based paint and hazards and provide "any records or reports available to the seller ... pertaining to lead-based paint and/or lead-based paint hazards" ([40 CFR 745.107](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/40/745.107)) [PRIMARY]; EPA and HUD read this to include even home test-kit results and records of hazards already fixed ([EPA/HUD Interpretive Guidance Part II, Q40–41](https://19january2021snapshot.epa.gov/sites/static/files/documents/igd.pdf)) [PRIMARY]. The listing agent "shall ensure compliance" ([40 CFR 745.115](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/40/745.115)) [PRIMARY]; knowing violators owe the buyer three times their damages ([42 U.S.C. 4852d(b)(3)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/4852d)) [PRIMARY] and face HUD penalties of up to **$22,263 per violation** since July 2025 ([Federal Register, 12 June 2025](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-06-12/html/2025-10519.htm)) [PRIMARY]. Any lead test in the package, and arguably any report line noting deteriorated paint that may contain lead, becomes a document the brokerage must make sure the buyer receives.

The brokerage's own knowledge is the third layer. The statutory agency disclosure form tells buyers that a seller's agent "should ... disclose all facts known to the agent materially affecting the value or desirability of property" ([RPL §443](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/443)) [PRIMARY]; the realtor guidance on the 2024 amendment confirms that licensees' duty "to disclose known material defects to prospective buyers" is "unchanged", and that licensees must not help the seller fill in the PCDS ([LIBOR PCDS FAQ](https://www.lirealtor.com/docs/default-source/default-document-library/PCDS-FAQ.pdf)) [SECONDARY]. A city court has treated what the seller's broker was told as the seller's own knowledge ([Kier v. Wilcox, Canandaigua City Ct. 2014, via NYSBA](https://nysba.org/pcda-amended-500-seller-credit-deleted-and-additional-questions-added-to-pcds/)) [SECONDARY; low precedential weight], and in a Putnam County case the Second Department let a fraud claim proceed against the real estate agents over their explanation of evidence of bats, while dismissing the inspectors, who had no duty to look for bats ([Jablonski v. Rapalje, 2005](https://static.case.law/ad3d/14/html/0484-01.html)) [PRIMARY]. A brokerage that funds, holds and summarises a report knows exactly what is wrong with the house, and its marketing words ("pre-inspected", "no major issues") are affirmative statements outside caveat emptor's shelter.

What, then, does a seller do with a report they dislike? There are four paths, and only three are lawful. They can fix and disclose the fix, the strongest position of all; disclose without fixing and let price or a credit absorb it; or decline to list. The fourth path, answering "No" or "Unknown" on the PCDS after reading the report, is a knowingly false statement and evidence of concealment (Simone). The deterrent effect is lopsided: owners of well-kept houses gain little and risk little, while the deferred-maintenance sellers that "fix everything first" is aimed at are the ones a careful attorney will advise not to create knowledge at all; downstate sellers' attorneys routinely paid the old $500 credit rather than answer the form's "vague questions and 'catch-all' provisions" ([NYSBA, Holtzschue](https://nysba.org/pcda-amended-500-seller-credit-deleted-and-additional-questions-added-to-pcds/)) [SECONDARY]. The inspection industry's own contract adds a cost sellers will not expect: if a seller shares the report and a buyer sues, the seller agrees to pay the inspector's legal fees ([InterNACHI agreement, clause 5](https://nachi.org/pre-listing-agreement.htm)) [PRIMARY]. If the father-in-law ever acted as a seller's lawyer while his firm brokered the sale (the provisional plan says he will not; Q4), he would hold the report as counsel owing confidentiality and as broker owing disclosure to buyers at once, which is one more reason the seller's own independent attorney should review the report against the PCDS. No data was found on how many sellers decline repairs after an inspection; a pilot would have to measure it.

## Who carries the risk, and what insurance pays

The table sets out each party's exposure under the structure as proposed and what brings it down.

| Party | Exposure | Legal hook | What contains it | What is left |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Inspector | Unlicensed practice | [RPL §§444-d, 444-n, 444-o](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf) [PRIMARY] | Current NY licence, checked against State data | None if licensed |

Insurance covers less of this than people assume. The only cover New York requires of an inspector is general liability of $150,000 per occurrence and $500,000 in aggregate ([19 NYCRR §197-1.1](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)) [PRIMARY]; a school's claim that New York also requires errors-and-omissions (E&O) cover ([AHIT](https://www.ahit.com/home-inspection-career-guide/home-inspector-errors-and-omissions-insurance)) [SECONDARY] is contradicted by the regulation. General liability pays for bodily injury and property damage, not for a wrong report, and some inspector policies carry an "INSPECTION, APPRAISAL AND SURVEY COMPANIES" exclusion that removes inspection work altogether ([11th Cir. opinion](https://media.ca11.uscourts.gov/opinions/unpub/files/201212327.pdf)) [SECONDARY]. E&O is the cover that answers a negligence claim, and it is cheap: the median quoted through one marketplace was **$989 a year for $1 million** (May 2026) ([Insureon](https://www.insureon.com/real-estate-business-insurance/home-inspectors/cost)) [SECONDARY]. The brokerage should require a certificate of E&O, not just the statutory certificate, before paying for any inspection. The brokerage's own E&O policy, which a small New York firm might buy for roughly $700–$2,000 a year ([TechInsurance, via WS4 notes](https://www.techinsurance.com/real-estate-insurance/agent-broker/cost)) [SECONDARY], was not shown to cover claims arising from an inspection programme the brokerage funds; that needs the carrier's written answer before launch.

Added up, an inspection plus radon for a village house on public water and sewer costs about **$550–$1,025**, and a rural house with a well, septic and termite report about **$1,100–$2,250**, before mold, lead or tank work [INFERENCE from the table; consistent with the WS4 notes]. The brokerage must pay whether or not the house sells, because the inspector's pay cannot depend on the sale ([§197-4.7(i)](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)) [PRIMARY]. Part of the spend may be repeated: Rockland requires the seller to order a certified well test when the contract is signed ([Rockland County Code ch. 389](https://ecode360.com/9669882)) [PRIMARY, via search snippet], Westchester has a similar law (WS2 notes), and VA and USDA buyers need fresh termite or well and septic reports near closing (see above). A pending bill would make a broad well test a non-waivable condition of every sale on a private well ([S9622, introduced March 2026](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/S9622)) [PRIMARY]. For a two-principal firm that must be cash-positive on each closing (kill criterion K7 in the WS5 notes), $1,000–$2,000 per listing paid up front is a real drag, and it is the largest single per-listing cost in the WS4 model [INFERENCE, WS4 notes].

On what it buys, the honest answer is that nobody knows. No study was found that isolates the effect of a pre-listing inspection on days on market, price or fall-through; the industry's claims ("Avoid 11th-hour negotiations", "justify your full asking price") come without data ([InterNACHI seller page](https://nachi.org/moveincertified)) [CLAIMED], and the newest franchise programme, Pillar To Post's "MarketReady" (March 2026), published no price or outcome figures ([RISMedia](https://www.rismedia.com/2026/03/31/pillar-to-post-home-inspectors-introduces-marketready-inspections/)) [SECONDARY]. (In New York an inspector "shall not determine the market value of the property or its marketability" ([19 NYCRR §197-4.4(e)](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)) [PRIMARY], so a "market-ready" label sits awkwardly here.) The nearest rigorous evidence is about disclosure laws, not inspections: state-mandated seller disclosure statements raised prices by about 3–4% across 291 metro areas in 1984–2004 ([Nanda and Ross](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2008-39.pdf)) [PRIMARY], a gain New York's now-mandatory PCDS should already capture [INFERENCE]. The problem the inspection targets is real: 14% of US home-sale agreements that went under contract in July 2026 fell through, and buyers are "more likely to walk away if an inspection uncovers problems" ([Redfin, 21 Aug 2026](https://www.placera.se/pressmeddelanden/with-buyers-firmly-in-the-driver-s-seat-home-purchase-cancellations-hit-highest-level-in-nearly-3-years-20260821)) [SECONDARY]; 16% of settlements were delayed in the three months to September 2026 ([NAR](https://www.nar.realtor/sites/default/files/2026-10/2026-10-realtors-confidence-index-10-13-2026_1.pdf)) [PRIMARY]. Whether a seller's report reduces those numbers is unmeasured, so it must not be claimed in marketing (§441-c "misleading advertising").

## Three designs, one recommendation

The brief asked for three designs compared on six dimensions. Design 1 is the proposal as stated: the friend inspects "everything" free for the seller, the brokerage arranges and holds the report, and the pitch is that buyers need not inspect. Design 2 is a narrowed lawful version: optional for the seller; the seller is the inspector's client; a licensed inspector with E&O cover, chosen from at least three (the friend may be one, with the relationship disclosed); the brokerage pays a fixed retail fee once the listing agreement is signed, owed whether or not the house sells; scope is the inspection plus radon, with water, septic and termite only where the house has them, up to a cap; the report goes to the seller only; the seller's own attorney reviews it against the PCDS before signing; "repair or disclose" is written into the listing agreement; the indemnity clause is struck; and nothing is said to buyers except, if the seller shares the report, a notice that it is not a substitute for their own inspection. Design 3 drops the free inspection and replaces it with something lighter: an inspection credit (the brokerage reimburses up to a set amount of a licensed inspection the seller orders, lawful as a consumer incentive per the [DOS FAQ](https://dos.ny.gov/real-estate-broker-frequently-asked-questions) [PRIMARY]), a disclosed list of at least three licensed inspectors with no payments, and optionally a home warranty for the buyer bought by the brokerage, which New York's insurance regulator has said a broker may do through a registered provider ([NY Insurance Dept. OGC Op. 05-12-02](https://www.dfs.ny.gov/insurance/ogco2005/rg051202.htm)) [PRIMARY].

| | (1) Proposal as stated | (2) Narrowed lawful version | (3) Drop or replace (credit, list, warranty) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Legal risk** | High: misdemeanor if the friend is unlicensed; a free favour is a RESPA "thing of value" and barred by §197-4.7(e); "you needn't inspect" is misleading advertising and, if S8729 passes, a §349 deceptive practice | Low to moderate: meets every rule found; residual risk because DOS has issued no guidance on brokerage-paid inspections | Low: incentives expressly permitted; warranty purchase approved by the insurance regulator; RESPA only bites if a warranty company pays the brokerage ([HUD 2010 rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-06-25/pdf/2010-15355.pdf)) [PRIMARY, via search snippet] |
| **Liability** | High: the brokerage holds the report (knowledge, §443, lead-paint agent duty) and invites buyer reliance (Burbach pattern) | Moderate to low: the brokerage holds nothing by default; if the seller shares, findings become known material facts the brokerage must treat as such | Lowest: the brokerage is not a party to the inspection |
| **Cost per listing** | $550–$2,250, more with mold, lead or tanks; up front, sale or no sale | $550–$1,025 village; capped (suggested $1,500) rural; up front once listed | $0 for the list; credit of a set amount (e.g. $400–$500); warranty about $766–$1,029 a year in NY ([This Old House](https://www.thisoldhouse.com/home-finances/home-warranty-new-york)) [SECONDARY]; both can be paid only on closed deals |
| **Seller appeal** | High headline appeal; falls once sellers' attorneys explain the knowledge consequence, especially for deferred-maintenance homes | Moderate: strongest for well-kept homes; some sellers decline after advice; still a tangible gift | Lower: the seller fronts or arranges the inspection; the warranty mainly pleases buyers |
| **Buyer effect** | Buyers still inspect; a minority might waive in hot markets, then sue on the seller's report | Buyers still inspect; possibly fewer renegotiation surprises (unproven) | None on inspections; a warranty comforts buyers without creating seller knowledge |
| **Demo value** | Looks impressive until a lawyer asks the first question | High: each checkpoint (licence check, disclosures, consent, attorney review, repair-or-disclose) is a visible, explainable step | Low to moderate: a settings screen and a list |

**Position: drop Design 1; launch Design 2 as a capped pilot; keep Design 3 as the fallback and as the offer for sellers who decline.** Design 2 keeps what is genuinely valuable in the father-in-law's idea (a seller who knows the house's condition before buyers do, and a concrete gesture of trust from an attorney-led firm) and removes the false promise to buyers that creates the liability. Run it on the first ten listings (about $10,000 at a $1,000 average), and measure what the research could not find: renegotiation rate, fall-through, days to contract against matched OneKey comparables, and how many sellers decline the inspection or decline repairs after it. If the pilot shows no difference, switch to Design 3, which costs little and carries almost no risk. The pitch line changes from "buyers won't need their own inspection" to "you'll know what a buyer's inspector will find before they do, and you decide what to fix".

The inspection step also shapes the product. In the demo it becomes a sequence of visible screens: an opt-in screen that explains in plain words that a report becomes something the seller must disclose; a live licence check against the State register that blocks booking an unlicensed inspector; an all-party disclosure of who pays and who knows whom, signed before booking; a scope builder that shows radon, water, septic and termite as separate services; a seller-only report vault with an access log; a human checkpoint where the seller's own attorney reviews the report against the PCDS (the software never pre-fills PCDS answers); a repair-or-disclose screen for each finding with no "ignore" option; an attachments tracker for the radon, mold, pest and lead documents; and a copy guard that blocks "pre-inspected", "no issues" and "no need to inspect" from any listing text. Each is listed with its reason in the WS6 handoff file for the traceability matrix.

## Conclusion

The free inspection was pitched as a buyer-side saving and turns out to be a seller-side preparation tool. That reframing matters beyond this feature: the evidence from England and Scotland shows that buyers trust a seller's paperwork only when its author is answerable to them, and New York's inspector statute points every duty at the client. Any part of the product that hopes buyers will rely on seller-produced material (condition summaries, AI-written highlight sheets, "verified" badges) runs into the same wall, and the same liability, so the product's whole posture toward buyers should be "here are the documents; get your own advice", which is also what keeps it on the right side of caveat emptor.

The more useful insight is that the legal machinery that sinks the proposal as pitched is exactly what makes the narrowed version demo well. A licence check against live State data, a three-party disclosure, an attorney checkpoint and a repair-or-disclose screen are concrete proof, in front of a New York lawyer, that the software knows where it must stop and hand over to a human. The open questions that still decide whether the perk earns its cost (whether the friend is licensed and insured, whether the firm's E&O policy covers the programme, and whether seller reports actually reduce renegotiation) are each answerable with one name, one letter to an insurer, and ten listings.
