## Key question 1: Market size and structure by county, 2025–2026, and the implied commission pool

### Takeaway
The eight target counties closed about 12,600 residential sales in 2025 on roughly $6.3 billion of volume (medians × counts), which at New York's current ~4.6–5% all-in commission implies a brokerage fee pool of roughly $290–320 million a year, excluding Westchester (another ~$265–285 million). Volume is ~30% below the 2021 peak, inventory is half of 2019, prices rose in every county in 2025, and 2026 year-to-date shows sales up mid-single digits with prices still rising in Dutchess and Ulster.

### Inferences
- Implied 2025 dollar volume and commission pool (closed sales × median price; rates from the New York figures in Key question 4): Columbia $364M → $16.8–18.2M; Dutchess $1,155M → $53–58M; Greene $226M → $10.5–11.3M; Orange $1,343M → $62–67M; Putnam $561M → $26–28M; Rockland $1,643M → $76–82M; Sullivan $282M → $13–14M; Ulster $775M → $36–39M. Eight-county total ≈ $6.35B of volume → ≈ $293M at 4.62% or ≈ $317M at 5.0%. Westchester ≈ $5.7B → $264–286M. [INFERENCE] Because averages run well above medians here (HVCR MLS average $581,523 vs median $460,000, ~26% higher; Houlihan's Westchester average $1.33M vs the $990K SF median), the true eight-county pool is plausibly $350–400M. [INFERENCE]
- The addressable pool for a "replace both agents" product is the ~95% of transactions that are agent-assisted on the sell side and ~90% on the buy side; the FSBO segment (5%) is at an all-time low nationally, so the product must take share from agents, not from do-it-yourselfers. [INFERENCE]
- Transaction counts in the two data families differ (Pattern/NYSAR counts all residential types across all MLSs; HGAR/OneKey counts single-family in OneKey only), so use Pattern for county totals and HGAR/OneKey for speed and pricing detail. [INFERENCE]
- Market speed is sharply tiered: Westchester/Rockland/Putnam sell in 40–52 days at or above list, Orange/Dutchess in ~58–60 days, Ulster 67–72, Columbia/Greene (HVCR MLS) 80–83, Sullivan 90 at 93% of list. Months of supply ranges from 1.2 (Westchester) to 6.5–6.7 (Sullivan, HVCR MLS). A listing product's value proposition differs by tier: in the southern counties pricing and exposure do the work; in the northern counties marketing and patience matter more. [INFERENCE]

## Key question 3: Discount, flat-fee, online and AI competitors actually serving these counties

### Takeaway
The region has no visible discount or AI-first brokerage with a physical footprint north of Westchester: Redfin's only licensed office in the nine counties is in White Plains (68 licensees), national flat-fee MLS services (Hauseit, Houzeo, UnoFee, ISoldMyHouse, nyflatfee) list Hudson Valley counties but operate remotely through OneKey MLS, several national players (Beycome, Help-U-Sell, Assist-2-Sell) do not operate here at all, and the only locally licensed flat-fee entity found is a one-broker shop in Walden.

## Key question 4: Local commission practice and buyer-broker compensation after the NAR settlement

### Takeaway
No Hudson Valley-specific commission dataset exists in public; the best New York-level evidence (an agent survey) puts the all-in rate at about 4.6% (2.4% listing, 2.2% buyer side), down from about 5.4% in 2024, while national closed-sale data show buyer-agent pay steady to rising at about 2.4–2.7%, most sellers still offering it, and the only rigorous evidence on low-fee listings (Massachusetts) shows they sell less often and more slowly.

### Cited Findings
- Clever's August 2026 survey of 434 agents: New York total commission 4.62% (listing 2.41%, buyer's agent 2.21%), down from 5.36% (2.72% / 2.64%) in 2024, the largest drop of any state; national averages 5.46% (2.76% / 2.70%) in 2026 vs 5.32% in 2024; 46% of agents say sellers still commonly offer to pay the buyer's agent. A separate July 2026 survey of 500 recent sellers: 35% offered to cover the buyer's agent commission; 45% did not know the requirement had been removed. — [Clever, "Is a 6% commission still standard?"](https://listwithclever.com/real-estate-blog/6-percent-real-estate-commission-explained/) [SECONDARY; small state sub-sample, agent-reported]
- Redfin closed-sale data, Q2 2025: average buyer's agent commission 2.43% nationally, up from 2.38% a year earlier and from 2.36% when the rules changed in August 2024; 2.52% under $500K, 2.34% at $500K–$1M, 2.21% at $1M+; RealTrends' tracker rose from 2.65% (Sep 2024) to 2.71% (Aug 2025). — [HousingWire, 12 Aug 2025](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/redfin-agent-commissions-q2-2025-post-nar-settlement/) [SECONDARY reporting primary data]
- Local flat-fee operator guidance, which reflects what the OneKey market expects: UnoFee tells sellers to set buyer-agent commission "at 2%–3%"; Hauseit says "90% of buyers [are] represented by agents" and the seller pays only the buyer-agent commission it chooses to offer. — [unofee.com](https://unofee.com/), [Hauseit](https://www.hauseit.com/agent-assisted-fsbo/) [CLAIMED]
- A 2017 Hauseit note on the Dutchess MLS said participants had to offer cooperating brokers "some form of commission, regardless of how much"; that pre-dates the 2024 rule change that removed compensation offers from MLS displays. — [Hauseit, Dutchess County MLS](https://hauseit.medium.com/the-dutchess-county-mls-explained-614015b3b9b2) [SECONDARY, outdated]. Beycome's page states the post-August-2024 norm: offers of buyer-agent compensation "can't appear on the MLS; it goes in a separate written agreement". — [beycome.com](https://www.beycome.com/flat-fee-mls/new-york) [CLAIMED]
- Steering evidence (not local): Barwick, Pathak and Wong, 653,475 eastern-Massachusetts listings 1998–2011: properties with lower commission rates were about 5% less likely to sell and took about 12% longer; the authors attribute it to buyer agents showing them less; large-share offices bought disproportionately few low-commission homes. Published AEJ: Applied Economics 9(3), 2017. — [NBER w21489](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21489) [SECONDARY]
- Sellers' own weighting of price: only 4% of sellers named "agent's commission" as the most important factor in choosing an agent (reputation 35%, honesty 22%, friend/family 15%, neighborhood knowledge 11%). — [NAR 2025 Profile, Exhibit 7-7](https://www.lirealtor.com/docs/default-source/lean-in---pdfs/2025_profile_of_home_buyers_and_sellers_final_report_updated-(1).pdf?sfvrsn=bda6b60_1) [PRIMARY]
- Off-MLS pressure: on 20 Oct 2026 OneKey publicly rejected Compass's demand to change rules on sharing publicly marketed listings; OneKey cited Bright MLS/Drexel research that on-MLS homes sold for 17.5% more than comparable off-market homes and Zillow data that New York off-market sellers lost a median $13,749. — [OneKey press release, 20 Oct 2026](https://corporate.onekeymls.com/news/onekey-mls-rejects-compass-demand-defends-the-open-real-estate-marketplace) [PRIMARY release; the cited studies are third-party claims]

### Inferences
- A 2.2–2.5% buyer-side offer remains the working norm in OneKey counties; a listing that offers materially less, or none, would be the exception in a market where ~90% of buyers arrive with an agent. The Massachusetts result is the best available estimate of the penalty (−5% sale probability, +12% time), and nothing local contradicts it. [INFERENCE]
- The New York decline from 5.36% to 4.62% in Clever's survey, if real, means incumbents have already given back about 0.7 points; a product that saves "far less than 2.5–3% per side" is competing against a base that is drifting toward ~4.5%, not 6%. [INFERENCE]

### Gaps
- No HGAR/OneKey, HVCR MLS or NYSAR publication of average compensation offered or paid in these counties; no AccountTECH state data retrieved.
- No local evidence either way on steering or slower sales for low-fee listings in Hudson Valley MLSs; HGAR's trade paper (Real Estate In-Depth) was unreachable (503).
- Share of 2025–2026 Hudson Valley sales where the seller paid the buyer's agent: not measured anywhere public.

## Key question 5: How buyers and sellers here choose an agent, the role of attorneys, and the NYC buyer

### Takeaway
National data strongly support the founders' premise that selection is relationship-driven (two-thirds of sellers and 58% of buyers use a referral or a prior agent; 80% of sellers and 74% of buyers talk to only one agent), but the same data show the trust runs to a known agent, not to a cheaper process, and nothing found shows attorneys acting as a meaningful referral channel; the NYC second-home buyer, who dominates the higher-priced Dutchess/Columbia/Ulster segment by brokers' account, arrives through NYC brands and portals rather than local networks.

### Cited Findings
- Buyers (NAR 2025 Profile, transactions July 2024–June 2025): 43% found their agent through a friend, neighbor or relative (49% of first-time buyers); 15% used an agent they had used before (18% of repeat buyers); 7% inquired about a specific property online; 6% via a website; 7% referred by another agent; 5% met at an open house; 4% agent outreach; 2% from a sign; 1% social media. 74% interviewed only one agent (76% of repeat, 67% of first-time buyers). 88% bought through an agent; 91% would use the agent again or recommend them; 62% already had. — [NAR 2025 Profile, ch. 4, Exhibits 4-6, 4-8](https://www.lirealtor.com/docs/default-source/lean-in---pdfs/2025_profile_of_home_buyers_and_sellers_final_report_updated-(1).pdf?sfvrsn=bda6b60_1) [PRIMARY]
- Sellers (same report): 37% referral, 29% used the agent before (66% combined); 5% agent outreach; 4% website; 4% referred by another agent; 3% open house; 2% direct mail; 80% contacted only one agent; 51% used the same agent to buy and sell (72% when the purchase was within 10 miles); 87% would definitely (75%) or probably (12%) recommend their agent; 65% already had. Most important factor: reputation 35%, honest/trustworthy 22%, friend or family 15%, neighborhood knowledge 11%, caring/listener 5%, commission 4%, firm 2%, technology 1%. Services wanted: marketing 23%, pricing 19%, selling within a timeframe 19%; 86% said their agent provided a broad range of services. — [NAR 2025 Profile, ch. 7, Exhibits 7-2, 7-7](https://www.lirealtor.com/docs/default-source/lean-in---pdfs/2025_profile_of_home_buyers_and_sellers_final_report_updated-(1).pdf?sfvrsn=bda6b60_1) [PRIMARY]; summarized by [BAM, 17 Nov 2025](https://nowbam.com/how-home-buyers-and-sellers-find-their-agents-in-2025/) [SECONDARY]
- Attorneys as a channel: the NAR exhibits list no "attorney" source for finding an agent (the residual "Other" is 7% for buyers and 11% for sellers). — [NAR 2025 Profile](https://www.lirealtor.com/docs/default-source/lean-in---pdfs/2025_profile_of_home_buyers_and_sellers_final_report_updated-(1).pdf?sfvrsn=bda6b60_1) [PRIMARY]. A Manhattan closing lawyer's account (2011) says lawyers do not pay brokers for referrals because New York ethics rules bar it. — [Brick Underground, 2011](https://www.brickunderground.com/blog/2011/02/inside_story_confessions_of_a_closing_lawyer) [CLAIMED, dated]
- Attorney-broker dual role: New York Real Property Law §442-f exempts "attorneys at law" from the broker-licensing article. — [RPL §442-f](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/442-F) [PRIMARY]. NYSBA Ethics Opinion 1015: a lawyer may act as both the seller's broker and lawyer in the same transaction only if the broker fee is fixed, non-refundable and not contingent on closing, and only with informed consent confirmed in writing; earlier opinions (208, 244, 291, 340, 493, 753, 919, 933, 1013) barred the combination when the commission was contingent because a broker paid only at closing "cannot be fully independent in advising the client as a lawyer"; broker services delivered from the same office are subject to the full ethics rules and the combined fee "may not be excessive". — [NYSBA Opinion 1015](https://nysba.org/ethics-opinion-1015/) [PRIMARY]
- NYC/second-home buyers: Corcoran Country Living's principal said in 2020 that "the vast majority of all sales transactions over $750,000" in the Hudson Valley involve an NYC-based buyer or seller. — [Corcoran release, 2 Jul 2020](https://inhabit.corcoran.com/corcoran-arrives-in-new-yorks-hudson-valley-with-its-sixth-affiliate-corcoran-country-living/) [CLAIMED]. Stefan Bolz (Hudson Dwellings Realty, Ulster; also an HVCR MLS director) said in June 2026 that 75–80% of his firm's clients come from NYC, Brooklyn, the Bronx, Long Island and New Jersey, that second-home purchasers are the dominant profile, relocations about 5%, and local buyers 30–40% "and shrinking"; a 2-bed Highland house listed at $550,000 drew six offers and closed above $600,000. — [KeyCrew, 19 Jun 2026](https://keycrew.co/journal/how-the-hudson-valley-became-one-of-new-yorks-most-sought-after-second-home-markets/) [CLAIMED, one firm]. Pattern for Progress (via Chronogram, Feb 2025): "thousands of households from the New York City metro area have moved north, often purchasing homes above asking price"; sustained second-home demand and short-term rentals tightened inventory. — [Chronogram, 11 Feb 2025](https://www.chronogram.com/river-newsroom/hudson-valley-home-prices-reach-new-heights-amid-tight-market-22793506/) [SECONDARY]. Pattern's 2026 report adds that "some households have likely moved back to the NYC metro area" and promises a 2026 study of corporate buyers and short-term rentals. — [Pattern for Progress 2025 annual](https://www.pattern-for-progress.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/2025-Q4-Annual-Housing-Report-.pdf) [PRIMARY]
- Local reputation dynamics: 51% of sellers used the same agent to buy and sell, rising to 72% for moves within 10 miles; a median seller has recommended their agent twice since selling; 24% recommended four or more times. — [NAR 2025 Profile, Exhibits 7-10](https://www.lirealtor.com/docs/default-source/lean-in---pdfs/2025_profile_of_home_buyers_and_sellers_final_report_updated-(1).pdf?sfvrsn=bda6b60_1) [PRIMARY]
- A Vassar content analysis found second-home references in 33% of 694 New York Times Hudson Valley articles (2002–2022), a measure of coverage, not sales. — [Musical Urbanism, 25 Sep 2025](https://pages.vassar.edu/musicalurbanism/2025/09/25/time-for-a-place-in-the-country-how-the-new-york-times-reports-the-hudson-valley-part-2/) [SECONDARY]

### Inferences
- The local-trust thesis is half-supported. Supported: selection is overwhelmingly by referral and repeat use, one conversation decides it, and reputation and honesty outrank commission 14:1 as the stated deciding factor, so a well-known local lawyer can plausibly win first conversations. Unsupported: there is no evidence that trust in a lawyer transfers to trust in a software process that removes the agent; the referred party in the data is a person who "provides a broad range of services" (86%), and sellers who received limited service were a small minority (14%). [INFERENCE]
- The ethics rules cut against the simplest version of "attorney-led": the founder cannot be the client's lawyer and earn a success-based commission in the same deal, and a flat non-contingent broker fee with written conflict consent is the only path NYSBA has approved. A fee that is flat and low fits that constraint, but then the lawyer's own legal representation in the deal is also inside the ethics perimeter (confidentiality, conflicts, "not excessive" combined fee), and attorneys may not pay or receive referral fees from brokers under the professional rules as reported. The legal advice function therefore has to be either separated or very carefully structured. [INFERENCE; confirm with counsel]
- For the NYC second-home buyer (the high-price segment that dominates Columbia, northern Dutchess and parts of Ulster), the relevant "trust" is in NYC brands (Compass, Corcoran, Sotheby's affiliates, Houlihan Lawrence) and in portals; a local lawyer's network does not reach that buyer, which removes the buyer side of the fee from the niche-play thesis in exactly the counties where fees per deal are highest. [INFERENCE]

### Gaps
- No Hudson Valley-specific survey of how sellers choose agents, and no evidence on attorneys as a referral source beyond the national exhibits (which do not list them).
- No measured share of NYC-origin buyers by county (deed-address analyses by Times Union or Pattern were not reachable this session).
- Whether RPL §442 permits a licensed broker to share a commission with an attorney-broker and how DOS treats attorney applicants (course/exam waiver) was not verified; the DOS broker page does not address attorneys, and the license-law PDF shows only the §442-f exemption text and a form field "Attorney, admitted to New York State Bar".

### Inferences
- Where entrenchment bites: (1) ancillary revenue (mortgage, title, insurance) lets the big three subsidize agent splits and marketing that a low-fee entrant cannot match; (2) referral entities and large sales forces mean the incumbents' own alumni and part-timers feed them listings; (3) in Putnam and Rockland, two firms hold a quarter to a third of licensees each; (4) Compass's private-exclusive push, if it ever succeeds, would hide the inventory a buyer-side product most needs. [INFERENCE]
- Where it does not: the associations and the Columbia/Greene/Ulster MLS are run by small-firm brokers, the MLS has consolidated into two open systems (OneKey; HVCR MLS) that a licensed entrant can join, and most of the 6,900 licensees in the eight counties close one to three sales a year, so they are not a coordinated defensive bloc. [INFERENCE]
- The single most entrenched asset is the referral habit itself (NAR data), which a lawyer-founder can enter but which rewards the person who shows up, not the software. The thesis therefore survives only if the lawyer is the visible service provider in each deal; it fails as a pure "software replaces agents" play. [INFERENCE]
