## Key question 1: Post-mortems — REX, Homie, Purplebricks (UK vs US), Redfin, iBuyers, Trelora, Door, Reali, Houwzer, Aalto, Alokee — and what analysts say killed them

### Takeaway
Every venture-scale US attempt to sell the same brokerage service for less has either died (REX, Reali, Purplebricks US, Zillow Offers, Homie's W-2 brokerage), been absorbed (Trelora), or converged back onto the traditional model (Redfin's move to commission-paid agents and sale to Rocket; Houwzer's drift from a $5,000 flat fee to "1%"; Purplebricks US dropping its upfront fee for a success fee). The recurring causes named by the record are (a) paid customer acquisition that never got cheap enough, (b) dependence on venture capital in a business whose unit economics are the same as any brokerage, (c) exposure to transaction-volume collapses (2022 rates), and (d) the inability to route around the buyer-agent/MLS system (REX). None of the named analysts attributes any failure primarily to consumers rejecting lower fees; several attribute it to the entrant having no real cost advantage.

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] The common thread is not consumer rejection of low fees but the absence of a cost advantage: a discounter still needs licensed humans for showings, pricing, negotiation and compliance, and still pays the buyer side. Once the Purplebricks and Homie models were adjusted to US reality (success fees, full service, co-broke), they were Redfin with less brand — and Redfin itself has retreated toward commission-paid agents.
- [INFERENCE] Paid acquisition is the killer at national scale (DelPrete's $15,000 vs $730 CAC). A local entrant that acquires through referrals avoids this specific failure mode, but inherits the others (thin per-deal margin, volume risk, buyer-side cooperation).
- [INFERENCE] REX is the clearest precedent for an "around the MLS" strategy: it lost in the market, abandoned the strategy, then lost in court on the theory that the rule was optional and Zillow acted independently.

## Key question 2: Steering evidence; REX v. Zillow/NAR; post-decoupling studies; Burnett/Sitzer and the NAR settlement; DOJ positions; the March 2025 Clear Cooperation change

### Takeaway
The best evidence (Massachusetts 1998–2011; 30 US markets 2021–22) shows low buyer-agent compensation measurably reduces a listing's odds and speed of sale, and that the mechanism is buyer-agent steering, not seller or listing-agent behaviour. The August 17, 2024 practice changes removed compensation offers from the MLS and required written buyer agreements, but did not ban seller-paid buyer-agent compensation, and measured commissions have been flat-to-up since. No peer-reviewed study yet measures steering after decoupling; the two private suits alleging steering boycotts (REX, Homie) both lost without reaching the merits of steering.

### Cited Findings

**Steering studies**
- Barwick, Pathak & Wong, "Conflicts of Interest and Steering in Residential Brokerage," AEJ: Applied 9(3), 2017: 653,475 listings, eastern Massachusetts, 1998–2011; buyer-agent commission rates largely uniform; lower-commission listings are 5% less likely to sell and take 12% longer; the effect "reflect[s] decreased willingness of buyers' agents to intermediate low commission properties (steering) rather than heterogeneous seller preferences or reduced effort of listing agents"; large agents/offices buy a disproportionately small share of low-commission listings; new brokerage offices charging low commissions grow more slowly than comparable higher-commission entrants. [PRIMARY abstract] — [NBER w21489](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21489); [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/114048); [AEA DOI 10.1257/app.20160214](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.20160214)
- Barry, Fried & Hatfield, "Et Tu, Agent? Commission-Based Steering in Residential Real Estate," 110 Iowa L. Rev. 1473 (May 15, 2025): ~265,000 listings in 30 US markets, June 2021–Feb 2022, with Redfin page-view data; lowest-commission group got 8% fewer page views, took 33% longer to sell, and was 51% less likely to sell at all than the going rate (the article's "typical market" estimate: 75% greater risk of not selling); even commissions slightly below the going rate were penalised. [PRIMARY abstract via journal page; magnitudes also in university summary] — [Iowa Law Review](https://ilr.law.uiowa.edu/volume-110-issue-4-1/2025/05/et-tu-agent-commission-based-steering-residential-real-estate); [UT McCombs](https://news.mccombs.utexas.edu/research/lowering-commissions-can-slow-home-sales/)

[PRIMARY disclosure] — [UT McCombs](https://news.mccombs.utexas.edu/research/lowering-commissions-can-slow-home-sales/)

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] Decoupling changed where the buyer-agent fee is written down (buyer agreement, seller concession, off-MLS offer) but not who ultimately funds it or how much it is. For a listing-side discounter, the steering risk documented by Barwick and Hatfield therefore still exists in practice: a seller who declines to fund 2.5% for the buyer's agent is still visibly "low-comp" to the buyer-agent community through off-MLS channels, agent networks and the buyer-agreement negotiation.
- [INFERENCE] Both private steering suits failed on procedure, not merits; an entrant should assume no legal remedy against informal steering and plan around it commercially (e.g., fund a market-rate buyer-side fee, or source unrepresented buyers).
- [INFERENCE] The delayed-marketing option mostly benefits large brokerages that can market internally; a small entrant has no in-house buyer pool and is therefore more, not less, dependent on MLS cooperation after March 2025.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or large-sample study measuring sale speed/probability of low-compensation listings after August 17, 2024 was found. The Fed paper and Redfin series measure rates, not steering.
- Share of transactions in which the seller still funds the buyer agent (2025–2026) was not found in an opened source; Redfin reports only "most."
- No Redfin commission data for any 2026 quarter was found (series may have paused after the Rocket acquisition — unknown).
- The Supreme Court certiorari outcome on DOJ's investigation and any 2025–2026 DOJ filings on buyer agreements or Clear Cooperation could not be verified from an opened source.

## Key question 3: Measured consumer behaviour 2025–2026 (NAR 2025 Profile, fee negotiation, willingness to use AI/flat-fee, stated vs actual)

### Takeaway
Revealed behaviour in the first post-settlement year is the opposite of the stated appetite for change: 91% of sellers and 88% of buyers used an agent, FSBO hit an all-time low of 5%, 74% of buyers and 80% of sellers talked to only one agent, two-thirds of sellers chose a referred or previously used agent, and only 4% of sellers named commission as the most important factor in choosing an agent. Meanwhile 37% of consumers *say* they would let AI complete a purchase with minimal human involvement. The gap between stated and actual behaviour is the single most important fact for a low-fee entrant.

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] The stated-vs-actual gap is large and persistent: 28% of FSBO sellers cite commission as the main reason, yet FSBO is 5% overall and 3% in suburbs; 37% say they would let AI buy, yet 1% of buyers found their agent via an app and 88% used an agent. Surveys of intent are not evidence of demand for a low-fee product; the only revealed-preference signal (FSBO) is shrinking.
- [INFERENCE] The behavioural lock-in is at the moment of agent *selection*: most consumers never compare (74%/80% talk to one agent) and choose on reputation/referral, not price. A low-fee entrant that is not already the referred name does not get into the consideration set; being cheaper does not create a comparison that never happens.
- [INFERENCE] The segment that actually buys limited service (7% MLS-only + 7% limited) is the realistic addressable market for a "software plus attorney" offer, not the 86% buying broad service.
- [INFERENCE] Negotiation is where buyers are least satisfied (78% very satisfied) and where a lawyer-led offer could credibly differentiate; but buyers rank negotiation as a top want only 13%/12% of the time — finding the home (50%) dominates.

### Gaps
- The 2025 Profile text I extracted does not report who paid the buyer's agent (seller, buyer, both) or the share of buyers who signed written agreements; I found no "compensation" tables in the PDF text.
- No credible survey specifically measuring willingness to use a *flat-fee or attorney-led* brokerage (as opposed to AI) was found.
- No Hudson Valley or New York-specific consumer data was found.

## Key question 4: Trust and local relationships — referral-driven markets, regional survivors (Hauseit), attorney-led brokerages, Purplebricks UK vs US

### Takeaway
Agent selection is a referral/reputation market everywhere, and more so for sellers; FSBO is twice as common in rural areas (10%) as nationally. The one durable regional low-fee model found, Hauseit, survives by being tiny in cost (flat $399–$1,199 upfront, no closing fee), by letting sellers keep paying buyer agents, and by not promising service — and it already lists Hudson Valley homes on OneKey MLS. New York ethics rules sharply constrain an attorney acting as broker in the same deal: a contingent commission is a non-consentable conflict; a fixed, non-refundable broker fee plus legal fee is permitted with written informed consent.

### Cited Findings

**Referral dominance and small-market behaviour**
- Sellers: 66% chose an agent by referral or prior use; 80% contacted only one; 51% used the same agent to buy and sell, 72% when moving within 10 miles; reputation (35%) and honesty (22%) dominate; commission 4%. Buyers: 43% referral, 15% prior agent, 74% interviewed one. [PRIMARY] — [NAR 2025 Profile PDF](https://www.rirealtors.org/clientuploads/documents/NAR/Homebuyers_and_sellers_trend_2025.pdf)
- FSBO share is highest in rural areas (10%) and resort areas (9%) and 6% in small towns, versus 3% in suburbs — the Hudson Valley spans all four categories. [PRIMARY] — [NAR 2025 Profile PDF, Exhibit 8-1](https://www.rirealtors.org/clientuploads/documents/NAR/Homebuyers_and_sellers_trend_2025.pdf)
- Barwick et al. found that newly established low-commission brokerage offices grow more slowly than comparable higher-commission entrants — i.e., the penalty attaches to the *firm*, not just the listing. [PRIMARY abstract] — [NBER w21489](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21489)

**Hauseit (NYC, Long Island, Hudson Valley, South Florida) — the surviving regional flat-fee model**
- Founded 2014; "pioneered the Agent Assisted FSBO model"; Hauseit is the listing broker of record, forwards inquiries, and then "its responsibilities end" — the seller runs showings, negotiation and closing and is told to hire an attorney; Base/Core plans include no phone, email or pricing support. [PRIMARY company site] — [Hauseit agent-assisted FSBO](https://www.hauseit.com/agent-assisted-fsbo/)
- Prices (one-time, upfront): $399 Base; $699 Core; $1,199 Pro (NYC); a $399 OneKey MLS upload add-on; markets: NYC (REBNY RLS), Long Island and the Hudson Valley (OneKey MLS), South Florida. Claims: "over 1,000" sellers and "$1 billion in listings since 2014." [PRIMARY site; traction CLAIMED] — [Hauseit](https://www.hauseit.com/agent-assisted-fsbo/)
- Buyer-agent fee: the seller chooses what to offer; with an unrepresented buyer the seller owes 0% at closing, with a represented buyer only the offered amount (example 2.5%). Hauseit tells sellers that "the 90% of buyers represented by agents" is why to list on the MLS at all; StreetEasy stopped displaying buyer-agent commission information in June 2024. [PRIMARY site] — [Hauseit](https://www.hauseit.com/agent-assisted-fsbo/)
- Inman profiled this "underground FSBO movement" in NYC in 2016 — the model has existed for a decade without becoming mainstream. [SECONDARY] — [Inman, Jul 25 2016](https://www.inman.com/2016/07/25/the-underground-for-sale-by-owner-movement-in-nyc/)
- Other regionals: Homie (Utah, >5% share claimed in 2020) wound down its W-2 brokerage in 2024; Trelora (Denver) was acquired in 2022; Houwzer (Philadelphia) survives as a multi-state "1%" brokerage, no longer flat-fee. (Sources in Q1.)

**Attorney-led brokerage in New York: law and ethics**
- NY Real Property Law §442-f: the broker-licensing article "shall not apply" to, among others, "attorneys at law." [PRIMARY statute] — [NY Senate, RPP §442-F](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/442-F)
- NY DOS's general broker requirements (age 20+, two years as salesperson or three in general real estate, 152 hours of qualifying education, 2.5-hour exam, $185 fee); the DOS pages I opened say nothing about attorney exemptions from course/exam, so the commonly stated attorney shortcut should be confirmed with DOS directly. [PRIMARY page; attorney treatment not shown] — [NY DOS: Become a Real Estate Broker](https://dos.ny.gov/become-real-estate-broker); [NY DOS: Real Estate Broker](https://dos.ny.gov/real-estate-broker)
- NYSBA Ethics Opinion 916 (Mar 27, 2012): a lawyer "may not offer to provide free legal services in a real estate transaction" where the lawyer is acting and being paid as broker; the broker's financial interest in closing "would create an influence that informed consent cannot relieve"; prior opinions 208 (1971), 291 (1973), 340 (1974), 493 (1978) and 752 bar acting as broker and counsel in the same transaction when paid a contingent commission. [PRIMARY ethics opinion] — [NYSBA Op. 916](https://nysba.org/ethics-opinion-916/)
- NYSBA Ethics Opinion 1015 (Aug 4, 2014): a lawyer may act as the seller's lawyer *and* broker only if the broker compensation is a fixed, non-refundable fee not contingent on closing, and the remaining personal-interest conflict is waived with informed written consent under Rule 1.7(b)(4); where legal and broker services are not "distinct" under Rule 5.7, "the combined fees for legal and broker services may not be excessive" under Rule 1.5(a). [PRIMARY ethics opinion] — [NYSBA Op. 1015](https://nysba.org/ethics-opinion-1015/)
- NYSBA Ethics Opinion 753 (Feb 26, 2002) applies the same logic to a lawyer-owned mortgage brokerage (not permitted for the buyer's or seller's lawyer) and lawyer-owned title abstract company (not permitted where it recommends insurability), because a broker paid only at closing "has a personal stake in the transaction." [PRIMARY ethics opinion] — [NYSBA Op. 753](https://nysba.org/ethics-opinion-753/)
- Precedent for the concept: in December 2009 attorney Joseph Ferrara announced "New York Real Estate Advocates," a residential brokerage run by real estate attorneys with licensed agents making the sales, modelled "after a law office somewhat," pitching savings on transfer taxes. I found nothing confirming it launched or survives. [SECONDARY; outcome unknown] — [The Real Deal, Dec 24 2009](https://therealdeal.com/new-york/2009/12/24/new-brokerage-modeled-on-law-firm-to-debut)
- Context on NY legal custom: flat fees of roughly $1,800–$3,000 are the norm for residential closings in NYC; buyers "often pick counsel because of a recommendation from a broker." [SECONDARY] — [Hauseit on lawyer fees](https://hauseit.com/real-estate-lawyer-fees-in-nyc); [NY Real Estate Journal column](https://nyrej.com/print/16879)

**Purplebricks: why the UK worked (for a while) and the US did not**
- UK enablers per the record: national TV brand ("Commisery," IPA award, 218% revenue growth), a single national market with 1.5–2% total commissions and a fixed upfront fee, and a booming market; failure factors: upfront fee regardless of sale (BBC Watchdog 2017; BBC 2024), review manipulation, and overseas losses; ended in a £1 sale (2023) and an HMRC winding-up petition (2026, withdrawn). [SECONDARY] — [Wikipedia: Purplebricks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purplebricks); [McCombs on UK vs US commission levels](https://news.mccombs.utexas.edu/research/lowering-commissions-can-slow-home-sales/)
- US-specific: $3,200 upfront fee in a market where buyers' agents expect 2.5–3% from the seller; expensive launch markets; ~$15,000 CAC; pivot to a success fee that erased differentiation. [SECONDARY, DelPrete] — [Property Industry Eye](https://propertyindustryeye.com/?p=69732); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purplebricks)

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] The UK precedent does not transfer: Purplebricks never had to fund a buyer's agent, so a flat fee could replace the entire seller cost. In the US the buyer side (2.4–2.5% measured) survives any listing-side discount, capping the headline saving at roughly half of what UK customers saw.
- [INFERENCE] Hauseit's longevity comes from having almost no cost to cover (no service) and from not fighting the buyer-agent system; it is evidence that a *very* cheap, no-promise listing product can persist regionally, not that a full replacement can.
- [INFERENCE] For an attorney-broker in New York, the ethics opinions push the design toward either (a) a fixed, non-refundable brokerage fee disclosed and waived in writing, with combined fees defensible as not excessive, or (b) a strict split in which the attorney is broker *or* counsel in a given deal, never both. A percentage success fee while also acting as counsel appears foreclosed. [INFERENCE from Opinions 916 and 1015; the attorney should confirm against current NY Rules and any later opinions.]
- [INFERENCE] The founder's genuine edge is being the referred name in a referral market (the attorney's network); the data say that edge is worth more than the discount itself (commission is the most important factor for 4% of sellers).

### Gaps
- No empirical study specific to small or semi-rural markets on how agents are chosen beyond the NAR location breakdown; no Hudson Valley market data (prices, days on market, OneKey rules) was gathered.
- No operating, attorney-led US brokerage with public results was found; the 2009 NYC example is unconfirmed.
- Hauseit's volumes and outcomes (days on market, sale prices vs agent-assisted) are company claims only.
- Whether a lawyer may own a separate brokerage that other licensees run while the lawyer's firm represents the same client (the "distinct services" structure Opinion 1015 alludes to) was not researched beyond the opinions above.

## Key question 5: The AI angle — what LLM-based buyer/seller agents can and cannot do today, early launches (2024–2026), and regulator/industry reactions

### Takeaway
As of October 2026 there is no evidence of any US product that replaces both agents end-to-end at scale. The funded "AI" entrants (Ridley) are sell-side software with on-demand licensed humans and tiny volumes (57 sales reported by Dec 2025); the incumbents' AI (Zillow's ChatGPT app, Realtor.com's RealAssist, Real's Leo Pro) is search and agent-productivity tooling. The industry's reaction has been to police MLS data use (IDX display rules, written authorisation for AI training) and to argue for human licensees in the loop; I found no state-regulator rule or documented incident specific to AI agents.

- 15% of consumers had used any AI tool for housing in the prior 12 months; top uses are neighbourhood research, search, valuation, summarising listings and mortgage questions — not negotiation or contracting. [PRIMARY survey] — [LendingTree, Jul 2026](https://www.lendingtree.com/home/mortgage/ai-housing-survey/)

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] What software demonstrably does today: search and matching, pricing guidance, checklists and document assembly, listing syndication, lead handling. What it does not do and cannot legally do alone in New York: open doors (lockbox/showing access runs through MLS licensees), hold fiduciary accountability, sign as a licensee, or practise law. In New York the contract is customarily drafted by attorneys anyway, so "AI drafts the contract" replaces a $1,800–$3,000 flat legal fee at most, not a 2.5–3% commission.
- [INFERENCE] The "both agents replaced" claim collides with the buyer side on day one: a represented buyer's agent will still expect to be paid, and an unrepresented buyer still needs showings, a lender, an inspector and (in NY) a lawyer. The entrant can remove the *listing* agent's percentage and *offer* an unrepresented-buyer path; it cannot remove the buyer agent from deals where one exists.
- [INFERENCE] Regulatory risk is currently low in volume but high in direction: MLS data-use rules already restrict AI products that touch IDX feeds, and the industry's declared position is "licensee in the loop." A design in which a licensed broker (the attorney) performs and signs every licensed act, with software as the workflow layer, fits the emerging norm; a design marketed as autonomous does not.

### Gaps
- No documented incident (complaint, enforcement, lawsuit) involving an AI buyer/seller agent in a US transaction was found; no state real estate commission guidance on AI and unlicensed practice was found, including for New York DOS.
- Modern Realty, Landian and Alokee (named in the brief as AI-era entrants) could not be verified: wrong/absent pages (YC page resolved to a different company; landian.ai DNS failure; alokee.com HTTP 522). Treat their status as unknown.
- No usage or review data for Realtor.com RealAssist or Zillow's ChatGPT app was found.

## Key question 6: What a Hudson Valley attorney-led software brokerage would need to beat the base rate, and explicit kill criteria

### Takeaway
The base rate for "cheaper agent" ventures is failure or reversion. The only ways the record shows to escape it are to have near-zero acquisition cost (referrals), to need no outside capital, to not fight the buyer-agent system, and to sell to the small segment that actually buys limited service. The following conditions and kill criteria are inferences from the evidence above; each names the evidence a citation-checker should test.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences

**Conditions required to beat the base rate** [all INFERENCE]
1. **Zero paid acquisition.** Listings must come from the attorney's existing referral network and repeat clients, because that is how the market selects agents and because paid CAC destroyed Purplebricks US and never worked for Homie. A plan with a marketing line item larger than a few percent of revenue is already off the evidence.
2. **Profitable per transaction from the first deal, with no venture capital dependency.** Every capital-dependent entrant died or reverted when volume fell (2022). A two-person firm can survive on a few dozen closings a year only if the price covers attorney time at market rates.
3. **Do not fight the buyer side; price the listing side.** Offer sellers a market-rate buyer-agent fee (or a clear, seller-chosen amount) so the listing is not "low-comp" to the buyer-agent community; take the saving from the listing-side 2.5–3%. Market the unrepresented-buyer path as an *option* that saves the seller more, not as the plan.
4. **Fixed, non-contingent fee if the attorney is both counsel and broker; otherwise separate the roles.** This is the structure NYSBA Opinion 1015 permits; a percentage success fee while acting as counsel is the structure Opinion 916 forbids. Confirm with DOS the licensing path and with current ethics counsel.
5. **Licensee-in-the-loop by design.** Every licensed act (listing agreement, showings access, offer presentation, negotiation) is performed or supervised by the attorney-broker; the software is a workflow and disclosure engine. MLS (OneKey/Hudson Gateway) data-license terms for any AI feature must be cleared in writing.
6. **Target the segment that already buys less service**, which in this geography is plausibly larger than the national 14%: rural/small-town FSBO is 6–10%, and 30% of FSBO sellers already know their buyer (an attorney sees many of those deals first).
7. **Measure steering locally.** Before scaling, compare days-on-market and sale probability of the firm's listings with matched OneKey listings at standard co-broke; if a penalty appears, raise the buyer-side offer rather than argue with the data.

**Kill criteria** [all INFERENCE; evidence named]
- **K1 — The economics only work if the buyer-agent fee disappears.** Kill if the model's margin depends on buyers being unrepresented, because 88% of buyers use agents, measured buyer-agent fees are 2.4–2.5% and rising post-settlement, and low-compensation listings carry a documented sale penalty (Barwick; Hatfield). Evidence: NAR 2025 Profile; Redfin Q3 2025; NBER w21489; Iowa L. Rev. 2025.
- **K2 — Listings must be bought with advertising.** Kill if, after the founders' network is exhausted (say the first 12 months), the pipeline requires paid lead generation at any scale. Evidence: DelPrete's CAC comparison; Homie's failed raise; Reali's shutdown.
- **K3 — The attorney intends to earn a percentage commission while also acting as counsel.** Kill (or restructure) because NYSBA Opinions 208/340/493/752/916 treat this as a conflict "informed consent cannot relieve"; only a fixed, non-refundable fee with written consent is sanctioned (Opinion 1015).
- **K4 — The product is pitched or built as autonomous.** Kill if the plan has software performing licensed acts without a human licensee signing each one, because MLS AI data rules already require written authorisation, NAR's stated position is MLS-compliance plus human review, and state regulators (who hold the power) have been publicly urged toward "licensee-in-the-loop" statutes. Evidence: NAR statement Oct 2025; NAR AI Policy Kit Mar 2026; HousingWire Sept 2026.
- **K5 — Customers come for price, not for the attorney.** Kill if intake shows most sellers found the firm by shopping fees rather than by referral or prior relationship: price-shoppers are 4% of the selection decision and are the customers Purplebricks and Homie had; referral customers are the ones every surviving agent has. Evidence: NAR 2025 Profile Exhibits 7-2, 7-7.
- **K6 — A local steering penalty persists after decoupling.** Kill the discount (not necessarily the firm) if the firm's listings at reduced buyer-side offers show materially longer time to contract or lower sale rates than matched OneKey comparables over a meaningful sample; the national evidence predicts exactly that. Evidence: Barwick et al.; Barry/Fried/Hatfield; Redfin agent reports.
- **K7 — The firm needs outside capital to reach break-even.** Kill because every capital-dependent precedent (REX $145m, Reali $292m, Homie, Purplebricks US) failed when volume or funding turned; a two-principal firm must be cash-positive on each closing.
- **K8 — The pitch relies on stated AI willingness.** Kill any projection built on "37% would let AI buy"; revealed behaviour is 5% FSBO (3% suburban), 1% of buyers finding agents through apps, and 15% having used AI for housing at all. Evidence: LendingTree 2026; NAR 2025 Profile.
- **K9 — The firm cannot get, or keep, MLS membership on acceptable terms.** Kill if Clear Cooperation, OneKey rules or IDX/AI data terms would force the product off-MLS; REX's off-MLS strategy failed commercially and in court (Ninth Circuit, Mar 2025), and Hauseit's survival depends on being *on* the MLS.

**What would make the base rate not apply** [INFERENCE]
- A fixed-fee legal-plus-brokerage bundle sold mainly to people who already trust the attorney (estates, divorces, relocations, FSBO sales between acquaintances — 30% of FSBOs), priced to be profitable at low volume, that pays buyer agents at market, that lists on OneKey, and that uses software only to make the attorney's hours cheaper. That is a better law practice with a brokerage licence, not a disruption; the evidence supports the former and not the latter.

### N3. The attorney channel: what NY ethics rules allow

### Takeaway
The rules let the attorney own a brokerage and tell people about it, but they bar him from being anyone's lawyer on a deal in which he has a stake in the commission, forbid taking closing referrals from his own brokerage's agents, and wrap any hand-off of his own law clients in written disclosures and consents. The provisional plan (he is broker of record and not either party's lawyer) is the structure the opinions support.
