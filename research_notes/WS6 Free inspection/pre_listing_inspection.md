## Key question 1 — NY law: who may inspect, what an inspection covers, the ethics rules, contracts, reports, insurance

### Takeaway
A paid home inspection of a 1–4 family dwelling in New York may only be done by a licensed NY home inspector (or a licensed architect/engineer or a code official acting within scope); the brokerage itself commits a misdemeanor if it "employs, permits or authorizes an unlicensed person" to inspect. The statutory "home inspection" excludes radon and pests and does not require septic, well-water, mold or code review, so a "comprehensive" upstate package is several separately contracted services. The inspector's duty runs only to the "client" (whoever engages him), the report is confidential without the client's consent, the fee may not be contingent on closing, and the inspector may not pay a brokerage anything for referrals or do repairs before closing. Only general-liability insurance ($150k/$500k) is required; errors-and-omissions cover is not.

### Cited Findings

**Licensing requirement and exemptions**
- [PRIMARY] RPL §444-d: "No person shall conduct or represent that he or she has the ability to conduct a home inspection for compensation unless such person is: 1. licensed as home inspector pursuant to this article; or 2. a person regulated by the state or a political subdivision thereof as an architect who is acting within the scope of his or her profession; or 3. a person regulated by the state or a political subdivision thereof as an engineer who is acting within the scope of his or her profession; or 4. a person who is employed as a code enforcement official by the state or a political subdivision thereof when acting within the scope of that government employment; or 5. a person making home inspections for the purpose of meeting the requirements of section four hundred forty-four-e of this article to qualify for licensure as a home inspector." — [NYS DOS, Home Inspection Professional Licensing law and regulations, compiled 03/2026 (PDF)](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf); section list at [nysenate.gov RPL Art. 12-B](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/A12-B)
- [PRIMARY] RPL §444-b definitions: "1. 'Client' means any person who engages or seeks to engage the services of a home inspector for the purpose of obtaining inspection of and written report about the condition of a residential building." "3. 'Home inspector' means a person licensed as a home inspector pursuant to the provisions of this article." "4. 'Home inspection' means the process by which a home inspector observes and provides a written report of the systems and components of a residential building including but not limited to heating system, cooling system, plumbing system, electrical system, structural components, foundation, roof, masonry structure, exterior and interior components or any other related residential building component as recommended or required by the department through regulation to provide a client with objective information about the condition of the residential building. The home inspector shall clearly identify in the written report which systems and components of the residential building were observed. A home inspection shall not include an inspection for radon or pests." — [DOS compilation](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)
- [PRIMARY] RPL §444-o (penalties): any person who "holds himself, herself, or itself out to the public as being able to engage in the business of home inspection without a license therefor ... or without a license to engage in the business of home inspection, directly or indirectly employs, permits or authorizes an unlicensed person to engage in the business of home inspection shall be guilty of a misdemeanor and upon the first conviction thereof shall be sentenced to a fine of not more than one thousand dollars; upon a second or subsequent conviction thereof shall be sentenced to a fine of not less than one thousand dollars nor more than five thousand dollars. Each violation of this article shall be deemed a separate offense." — [DOS compilation](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)
- [PRIMARY] RPL §444-n: the Secretary of State "may issue an order requiring restitution and/or directing the cessation of any activity for which a license is required" against anyone who "acted as, temporarily or otherwise, a home inspector within this state without a valid license." — [DOS compilation](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)
- [SECONDARY, from a search snippet of a 2007 Appellate Term decision, not opened] Licensing under Article 12-B became mandatory for inspections of one- to four-unit buildings effective December 31, 2005; the stated legislative aim was consumer protection for buyers who pay a third party to inspect. — [nycourts.gov 2007 NY Slip Op 50276(U)](https://www.nycourts.gov/reporter/3dseries/2007/2007_50276.htm)

**Duty of care, confidentiality and prohibited acts (statute)**
- [PRIMARY] RPL §444-g(1): "The duty of every home inspector shall be to the client." §444-g(3): "No later than five business days after the completion of a home inspection on behalf of a client, each home inspector shall provide such client with a written report of the findings of such inspection. ... Every such written report and the information contained therein shall be deemed confidential and shall not be disclosed without the express consent of the client; provided, however, that department representatives ... shall have access to such reports". — [DOS compilation](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] The "friend upstate" must hold a NY home inspector licence (or be a NY-licensed architect/PE acting within scope) the moment anyone pays him — and the brokerage paying is "compensation" under §444-d even if the seller pays nothing. If he is unlicensed, the brokerage itself is exposed under §444-o ("directly or indirectly employs, permits or authorizes an unlicensed person") and to a DOS cease-and-restitution order under §444-n; the brokerage's own licence is then at risk under RPL §441-c (see KQ2). A genuinely unpaid favour is a thin reed: §444-d also covers anyone who "represent[s] that he or she has the ability to conduct a home inspection for compensation", and a free inspection marketed by a brokerage that profits from the listing is hard to characterise as outside the "business of home inspection".
- [INFERENCE] "Comprehensive" is not what the statute delivers. The statutory inspection excludes radon and pests by definition (§444-b(4)) and is "not technically exhaustive" (§197-5.16(j)); septic, well-water quality, mold, oil tanks and code compliance are outside the minimum scope. A Hudson Valley "everything" package is therefore a bundle of separately scoped services (home inspection + radon test + water-quality lab test + septic evaluation/dye test + WDI report), each with its own contract, provider rules and price (see KQ5).
- [INFERENCE] Who the "client" is decides everything downstream. If the brokerage "engages" the inspector (§444-b(1)), the inspector's duty runs to the brokerage, not the seller (§444-g(1), §197-4.7(a)); the report is the brokerage's confidential document; and the seller becomes a "third party" who needs the brokerage's consent to see it (§197-4.3). If instead the seller is the client and the brokerage merely pays, §197-4.7(b)–(c) requires the inspector to disclose the arrangement in writing before accepting the job and all interested parties to agree to it, and §197-4.3 means the brokerage cannot receive the report without the seller's prior consent.
- [INFERENCE] The brokerage must pay the inspector's fee whether or not the house lists or closes: §444-g(4)(d) and §197-4.7(i) forbid any fee "contingent upon ... the closing of a real estate transaction". A "free if we get the listing" structure is unlawful for the inspector.
- [INFERENCE] Any economic quid pro quo running from the inspector to the brokerage (volume discount, free inspections funded by the inspector, exclusivity in exchange for referrals) is prohibited on the inspector's side by §444-g(4)(c) and §197-4.7(e) ("shall not directly or indirectly compensate, in any way, ... real estate brokerage companies ... for future referrals of inspections or for inclusion on a list of recommended inspectors or preferred providers or any similar arrangement"). A clean structure is the brokerage paying full retail price under a written agreement and receiving nothing from the inspector.
- [INFERENCE] The friend cannot fix what he finds before closing (§444-g(4)(a)); if the plan relies on him "making the fixes", that is barred for anything within the standards of practice.

## Key question 2 — Liability: inspector, seller (PCDS), brokerage

### Takeaway
Since 20 March 2024 every NY seller of a 1–4 family home must deliver a Property Condition Disclosure Statement answered "based upon your actual knowledge"; a pre-listing report converts "Unknown" into actual knowledge, so a seller who then answers falsely faces statutory "actual damages" for willful failure plus common-law fraud that an "as is" clause does not defeat (Simone v. Homecheck: a false "No" on the PCDS "may be proof of active concealment"). The inspector's contractual liability to his client can validly be capped at the fee for ordinary negligence (Smith-Hoy; Rector) but not gross negligence, and a buyer who is handed the report with the intent that she rely on it can satisfy New York's near-privity test (Ossining). The brokerage is protected by caveat emptor from mere silence (Serba v. Cook, 2026) but the §443 agency form says a seller's agent "should ... disclose all facts known to the agent materially affecting the value or desirability of property", DOS can discipline for "untrustworthiness", and summarising a report it holds ("pre-inspected, no major issues") is an affirmative representation that the doctrine does not protect.

### Cited Findings

**The Property Condition Disclosure Act after the 2024 amendment**
- [PRIMARY] RPL §461(3): "'Knowledge' means only actual knowledge of a defect or condition on the part of the seller of residential real property." §461(5): residential real property is a 1–4 family dwelling, excluding unimproved land, condominium units, cooperative apartments and HOA property not owned in fee simple. — [nysenate.gov RPL §461](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/461)
- [PRIMARY] RPL §462(1): the seller "shall complete and sign a property condition disclosure statement" and deliver it "prior to the signing by the buyer of a binding contract of sale"; the statement is attached to the contract; the section allows parties to agree otherwise on physical condition, including "as is" sales. §462(2) prescribes the form, which states: "THIS DISCLOSURE STATEMENT IS NOT A WARRANTY OF ANY KIND BY THE SELLER" and "IT IS NOT A SUBSTITUTE FOR ANY INSPECTIONS OR TESTS", and warns that a knowingly false or incomplete statement may expose the seller to buyer claims before or after title transfers; the statutory form contains 56 numbered questions. §462(3): "Nothing in this article shall require a seller to undertake or provide for any investigation or inspection" of the property or public records. No $500-credit language appears in the current text (page revision 2025-07-04). — [nysenate.gov RPL §462](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/462)
- [PRIMARY] RPL §465(1): "Nothing contained in this article shall be construed as limiting any existing legal cause of action or remedy at law, in statute or in equity." §465(2): a seller who provides a PCDS, or provides or fails to provide a revised one, "shall be liable only for a willful failure to perform the requirements of this article", and for such a failure "shall be liable for the actual damages suffered by the buyer in addition to any other existing equitable or statutory remedy." — [nysenate.gov RPL §465](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/465)
- [PRIMARY] RPL §466 (Duty of an agent): a listing agent "shall have the duty to timely inform each seller represented by that agent" of the seller's obligations; a buyer's agent (or a seller's agent dealing with an unrepresented buyer) must inform the buyer of the buyer's rights and obligations "before the buyer signs a binding contract of sale"; an agent who does so "shall have no further duties under this article" and is not liable to any party for a violation of it. — [nysenate.gov RPL §466](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/466)
- [SECONDARY] Karl Holtzschue (NYSBA Real Property Law Journal, Dec 2023; posted 28 Feb 2024): Chapter 484 of the Laws of 2023 (signed 22 Sept 2023, effective 20 March 2024) deleted the $500 credit, added seven flood questions (49 → 56 questions), retitled §465 from "Remedy" to "Liability" and repealed §467. "The statute no longer addresses a remedy or liability for the failure of the seller to provide a fully completed PCDS." Possible consequences he lists: rescission, a suit to compel delivery ("costly and time-consuming and probably not very practical"), a defence to anticipatory breach, support for superior-knowledge/special-facts claims, or evidence of active concealment. He notes downstate sellers' attorneys had routinely advised paying the $500 instead of answering "vague" questions with "catch-all" provisions. On fraud: "Neither the 'as is' nor merger clauses will generally protect the seller from a claim of fraud." Cases he cites: Malach (seller who answered "unknown" not liable absent actual knowledge); Kier (broker's knowledge imputed to the seller and treated as concealment); 470 4th Avenue (an "as is" clause did not bar fraud where sellers refused inspection behind walls). — [NYSBA: PCDA Amended; $500 Seller Credit Deleted](https://nysba.org/pcda-amended-500-seller-credit-deleted-and-additional-questions-added-to-pcds/)
- [SECONDARY] NYSBA news, 20 March 2024 (Jennifer Andrus): quoting Holtzschue, "The buyer has a duty to use the means available to check the condition of the property" and "Buyers lose many cases when they fail to do so." — [NYSBA: New Rules on Property Condition Disclosure and Flood Risk Go Into Effect Today](https://nysba.org/breaking-news-new-rules-on-property-condition-disclosure-and-flood-risk-go-into-effect-today/)
- [SECONDARY] Farrell Fritz (Laura K. Fallick, 5 Feb 2024): the legislature found "a $500 penalty is an insufficient substitute for providing critical information to a homebuyer"; sellers "must provide the PCDS or risk being held liable for the failure to do so." — [LI Land Use & Zoning blog](https://www.lilanduseandzoning.com/2024/02/05/disclosure-revolution-legislation-makes-property-condition-disclosure-statements-mandatory-adding-flood-risks-and-waving-farewell-to-the-500-credit/)

**Caveat emptor, "as is", active concealment (seller and seller's agent)**
- [PRIMARY] Stambovsky v. Ackley, 169 A.D.2d 254 (1st Dept 1991): "New York adheres to the doctrine of caveat emptor"; "the real estate broker, as agent for the seller, is under no duty to disclose to a potential buyer" the house's reputation; "Where a condition which has been created by the seller materially impairs the value of the contract" and is peculiarly within the seller's knowledge, "nondisclosure constitutes a basis for rescission as a matter of equity"; "Even an express disclaimer will not be given effect" where the facts are "peculiarly within the knowledge of the party invoking it"; remedy was rescission and return of the down payment, not fraud damages. — [static.case.law, 169 A.D.2d 254](https://static.case.law/ad2d/169/html/0254-01.html)
- [PRIMARY] Simone v. Homecheck Real Estate Servs., Inc., 42 A.D.3d 518 (2d Dept 2007): sellers answered "No" on the PCDS to questions about footings, water damage, flooding, drainage, seepage, plumbing, foundation and radon; the buyer (who hired his own inspector, Homecheck, which found no material defect) later found leaks, a sinking deck, mold, a cracked chimney and rot. Held: "New York adheres to the doctrine of caveat emptor and imposes no liability on a seller for failing to disclose" and "The mere silence of the seller, without some act or conduct which deceived the buyer" is not fraud, but a false statement in a PCDS "may be proof of active concealment", so the fraud claims against the sellers survived; the breach-of-contract claim was dismissed because the contract sold the property "as is" without warranty as to condition. — [static.case.law, 42 A.D.3d 518](https://static.case.law/ad3d/42/html/0518-01.html)
- [PRIMARY] Masters v. Visual Bldg. Inspections, Inc., 227 A.D.2d 597 (2d Dept 1996): buyers sued the inspector (who had reported a leak-free roof) and sellers; against the sellers, "a specific disclaimer defeats any allegation that the contract was executed in reliance" on contrary representations, and a rider clause that the roof representation "shall not survive the later of closing or delivery of possession" barred the fraud claim; the action against the inspector was severed and not decided. — [static.case.law, 227 A.D.2d 597](https://static.case.law/ad2d/227/html/0597-01.html)
- [SECONDARY, summarising a June 2026 Second Department decision] Serba v. Cook, 2026 N.Y. Slip Op. 03464 (2d Dept, 3 June 2026): buyer discovered after closing that the house was not connected to the public sewer and sued the seller, title company, brokerage, its managing member and the listing agent; dismissal affirmed because the complaint "failed to adequately allege facts that would support a finding" that the seller or real-estate defendants thwarted the buyer's diligence, and the fraud claims "are barred by the 'as is' clause in the contract and the specific disclaimer." The firm's summary states the general rule that "a seller has no general duty to volunteer information about defective conditions in the property" and collects Daly v. Kochanowicz, 67 A.D.3d 78; Jablonski v. Rapalje, 14 A.D.3d 484; Razdolskaya v. Lyubarsky, 160 A.D.3d 994; Gordon v. Connie Profaci Realty, 231 A.D.3d 712 (2d Dept 2024); Venezia v. Coldwell Banker Sammis Realty, 210 A.D.2d 480; Danann Realty v. Harris, 5 N.Y.2d 317; Basis Yield Alpha Fund v. Goldman Sachs, 115 A.D.3d 128 (a disclaimer bars fraud only if specific to the misrepresented fact and the fact is not peculiarly within the other party's knowledge). The nycourts.gov text of Serba could not be retrieved (403). — [Fox Horan & Camerini LLP summary](https://www.fhnylaw.com/caveat-emptor-in-an-as-is-world-fraud-in-the-purchase-and-sale-of-real-property)
- [SECONDARY, search snippet of a 2026 Supreme Court decision, not opened] "The doctrine of caveat emptor imposes no liability on a seller [or the seller's agent] for failing to disclose information regarding the premises when the parties deal at arm's length"; where the buyer could have learned the truth through ordinary diligence, the buyer "will not be heard to complain". — [nycourts.gov 2026 NY Slip Op 30635(U)](https://www.nycourts.gov/Reporter/pdfs/2026/2026_30635.pdf)

**Inspector liability: limitation clauses, gross negligence, non-clients**
- [PRIMARY] Smith-Hoy v. AMC Prop. Evaluations, Inc., 52 A.D.3d 809 (2d Dept 2008): buyers paid $485 for a HouseMaster pre-purchase inspection under a contract limiting liability to the fee. "A clear contractual provision limiting damages is enforceable absent a special relationship between the parties" (Colnaghi, U.S.A. v Jewelers Protection Servs., 81 N.Y.2d 821; Sommer v Federal Signal Corp., 79 N.Y.2d 540, 553), absent "a statutory prohibition, or an overriding public policy"; "a party may not limit its liability for damages caused by its own grossly negligent conduct", but the inspector's "alleged failure to properly conduct the inspection does not rise to the level of gross negligence" (Clement v Delaney Realty Corp., 45 A.D.3d 519), so damages were capped at the fee; vicarious-liability claims against the franchisor were dismissed. — [static.case.law, 52 A.D.3d 809](https://static.case.law/ad3d/52/html/0809-01.html)
- [PRIMARY] Rector v. Calamus Group, Inc., 17 A.D.3d 960 (3d Dept 2005) (upstate department): $205 inspection fee; clause in bold capitals limiting liability to the lesser of repair cost or the fee; "[a] clear contractual provision limiting damages is enforceable absent a special relationship"; "a party may not insulate itself from damages caused by its own grossly negligent conduct" but none was shown; "the agreement limiting Hometeam's liability under that contract to the $205 inspection fee is enforceable"; the co-buyer who did not sign, as a third-party beneficiary, "is bound by the terms of that contract, including the limitation of liability provision." — [static.case.law, 17 A.D.3d 960](https://static.case.law/ad3d/17/html/0960-01.html)
- [SECONDARY, search snippet, not opened] Caropreso (Sup. Ct. 2009, 2009 NY Slip Op 31197(U)): a motion to cap an inspector's liability at the fee was denied because the contract contained no such limiting language. — [nycourts.gov](https://www.nycourts.gov/reporter/pdfs/2009/2009_31197.pdf)
- [PRIMARY] Ossining Union Free School Dist. v. Anderson LaRocca Anderson, 73 N.Y.2d 417 (1989) (Kaye, J.): engineers retained by the district's architect, with no contract with the district, could be sued by the district for negligent misrepresentation; the Credit Alliance criteria require (1) awareness that the reports were to be used for a particular purpose, (2) reliance by a known party in furtherance of that purpose, and (3) "some conduct by the defendants linking them to the party or parties" evincing the defendants' understanding of that reliance; "Nor does the rule apply only to accountants." — [static.case.law, 73 N.Y.2d 417](https://static.case.law/ny-2d/73/html/0417-01.html)
- [CLAIMED, InterNACHI member forum] A member described program language under which persons who view a seller's report (buyers, agents) "are not specifically intended beneficiaries" of the agreement, plus a seller indemnity for third-party claims; the thread I could open did not reproduce any such clause, and Nick Gromicko's posts say only to "give your client (the seller) your report". Treat as unverified. — [forum.nachi.org Move In Certified thread](https://forum.nachi.org/t/move-in-certified-question/37646)

**The brokerage's duties and exposure**

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] The PCDA's protection for sellers is the word "knowledge" = "only actual knowledge" (§461(3)) plus "no duty to inspect" (§462(3)). A brokerage-sponsored inspection deliberately removes that protection: every item in the report becomes something the seller actually knows, the form must be answered accordingly ("Any known material defects?"), and a later "No"/"Unknown" is a knowingly false statement — exposure to §465(2) actual damages (willful failure), to common-law fraud/active concealment that "as is" and merger clauses do not bar (Simone; Holtzschue), and to the revision duty if the report arrives after an earlier PCDS was given. In Holtzschue's Kier example a broker's knowledge was imputed to the seller; a brokerage-held report is the clearest possible case of that imputation.
- [INFERENCE] Because test results are attachments (Q19, Q19-a, Q23: "If yes, attach report(s)"), a pre-listing radon, mold or termite test done under the programme becomes a document the seller must hand to every buyer who gets the form. The structural/mechanical questions do not require attaching the home-inspection report, but a seller who answers "Yes, known defect — see attached" is the least risky path and effectively publishes the report.
- [INFERENCE] The inspector's limitation-of-liability clause works against his client for ordinary negligence (Smith-Hoy; Rector, which is Third Department — the Hudson Valley's Albany-side counties — while the Second Department covers Westchester/Putnam/Dutchess/Orange/Rockland), not for gross negligence, and only if the clause is actually in the contract (Caropreso). Whether a buyer-stranger is bound by the seller's contract is unsettled: Rector bound a non-signing co-client as a third-party beneficiary, which cuts both ways — a buyer who is handed the report "to rely on" may be treated as an intended beneficiary (bound by the cap) or as a non-client to whom the Ossining near-privity test applies (awareness of purpose + known reliance + linking conduct such as hosting the report for buyers). Programme design (report addressed "for the seller only", no buyer-facing hosting, express non-reliance notice) is what moves the inspector in or out of near-privity.
- [INFERENCE] Insurance: the only mandatory cover is $150k/$500k general liability, which typically responds to bodily injury/property damage, not to a negligent report. If the friend has no E&O policy, a negligence claim above the fee cap (gross negligence) or a near-privity claim by a buyer is effectively uninsured, and plaintiffs' counsel will look to the brokerage as the deeper pocket.
- [INFERENCE] Brokerage exposure comes in three layers: (1) regulatory — the §443 form's "should ... disclose all facts known to the agent materially affecting the value or desirability of property" sets DOS's expectation; sitting on a report of known defects while marketing the house invites a §441-c "untrustworthiness" complaint, which does not require a court to find fraud; (2) tort — caveat emptor protects mere silence by a seller's agent (Stambovsky; Serba), but a sanitised "Home Highlights"-style summary, a "pre-inspected" badge or an MLS remark like "inspected, no major issues" is an affirmative statement and a partial disclosure, which is where fraud and negligent-misrepresentation claims against brokers succeed; (3) programme — if the brokerage is the inspector's client, the report is the brokerage's own confidential document (§444-g(3)), which maximises its knowledge and minimises its deniability.
- [INFERENCE] RESPA is a question to park with the attorney, not a blocker: a pre-listing inspection is not "required by ... the sales contract or mortgage documents", and a brokerage paying full price for a service it gives away is not receiving a "thing of value" for referrals; the risk arises only if the inspector discounts or kicks back in exchange for the brokerage's referral stream (which Article 12-B already forbids).

### Gaps
- No New York appellate decision was found in which a buyer sued the seller's (or a brokerage's) pre-listing inspector; the near-privity analysis above is by analogy (Ossining) and is my inference, not settled law.
- Serba v. Cook (2026) and the 2026 Supreme Court decision were read only through a law-firm summary and a search snippet; the nycourts.gov texts returned 403.

## Key question 3 — Buyer behaviour: do buyers skip their own inspection when the seller provides one?

### Takeaway
Roughly one buyer in five waives the inspection contingency (NAR: 21% in September 2025, 17% a year earlier; monthly 2025 readings 18–25%), but no 2021–2026 survey was found that measures whether buyers accept a seller-provided report in place of their own; the only quantified evidence is an inspector's 2020 Indiana anecdote that buyers still order their own inspection about 80% of the time, and the 2026 trade advice is that "your buyer may still order their own inspection". FHA tells buyers in writing that an appraisal "does not replace a home inspection"; the New York bill to ban waiver conditions (S5772) was stricken in April 2026.

## Key question 5 — Economics: what a comprehensive upstate inspection costs and what the brokerage would be giving away

### Takeaway
A standard home inspection in New York runs about $350–450 (Fixr, Jan 2025; national average $400), but the add-ons that make an inspection "comprehensive" in the Hudson Valley roughly double or triple it: radon ~$85–400, well-water testing ~$250–500, septic evaluation ~$300–650 plus $150–250 for a dye test, WDI ~$85–150 — so a full package is on the order of $900–1,800 per listing before re-inspections, and the brokerage must pay it whether or not the listing sells. Because most buyers still inspect, the seller's economic benefit is "no surprises", not avoiding a second inspection.

### Cited Findings

- [INFERENCE] The buyer-side saving is mostly illusory: with ~80% of buyers inspecting anyway (anecdotal) and buyers' counsel advising them to, the seller's realised benefit is early knowledge (repair at leisure, price accurately, fewer renegotiations), which is worth something but is not "no second inspection".
- [INFERENCE] Marketing the programme as a reason for buyers to skip inspection is itself a liability driver (see KQ2): the brokerage would be inviting reliance on a report it paid for.

## Key question 6 — The seller-reluctance problem: sellers who learn of defects but will not fix them

### Takeaway
In New York the choice after a pre-listing inspection is binary — repair or disclose — because the report converts "Unknown" into actual knowledge that the PCDS and the fraud cases make unlawful to hide; "as is" pricing and credits remain available but only on top of disclosure. The downstate practice of avoiding the PCDS entirely (via the now-abolished $500 credit) because its questions were "vague" with "catch-all" provisions shows that risk-averse sellers' attorneys dislike creating seller knowledge, which is the mechanism by which the programme deters the sellers it most needs.

### Cited Findings
- [PRIMARY] RPL §461(3) ("only actual knowledge"); §462(1) (parties may agree on "as is"); §462(2) form (not a warranty; knowingly false or incomplete statement exposes seller; seller may attach additional pages); §462(3) (no duty to inspect); §465(2) (willful failure → actual damages); form certification (duty to deliver a revised statement "as soon as practicable" on acquiring knowledge that makes a prior statement materially inaccurate, but not after transfer of title or buyer occupancy). — [RPL §461](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/461); [RPL §462](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/462); [RPL §465](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/465); [DOS form](https://dos.ny.gov/property-condition-disclosure-statement)
- [PRIMARY] Simone v. Homecheck: a false "No" on the PCDS "may be proof of active concealment"; the "as is" clause defeated only the contract claim, not fraud. — [42 A.D.3d 518](https://static.case.law/ad3d/42/html/0518-01.html)
- [SECONDARY] Holtzschue: downstate sellers' attorneys had advised paying the $500 credit instead of completing the form "because of vague questions and 'catch-all' provisions"; sellers "should carefully consider" explaining answers and attaching pages; he raises whether a buyer's waiver of PCDS delivery would be enforceable, suggesting a court "may well view the public policy of consumer protection" as outweighing freedom of contract. — [NYSBA](https://nysba.org/pcda-amended-500-seller-credit-deleted-and-additional-questions-added-to-pcds/)
- [SECONDARY] HomeLight (Jan 2026): "state laws mandate that sellers disclose known property issues to buyers"; sellers must disclose any pre-listing findings they choose not to fix; agents use findings to set price; buyers often ask for repair credits. — [HomeLight](https://www.homelight.com/blog/pre-listing-home-inspection/)
- [CLAIMED] InterNACHI: "Repairs can be made ahead of time"; the client can "correct any misstatements in the inspection report" before distribution; the programme does not describe what happens when the seller declines repairs. — [nachi.org/moveincertified/agents](https://www.nachi.org/moveincertified/agents)
- [PRIMARY] The seller (if client) controls release of the report (§444-g(3); 19 NYCRR §197-4.3), and the inspector may disclose "immediate threats to health or safety" to the owner/occupants under the mandatory contract clause (§197-4.2(a)). — [DOS compilation](https://dos.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/03/home-inspection-professional-licensing-law_03.2026.pdf)
- [PRIMARY] The seller's agent, in dealings with the buyer, "should ... disclose all facts known to the agent materially affecting the value or desirability of property" (RPL §443(4)(a) form). — [RPL §443](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/443)
- [SECONDARY] NYSAR opposed the mandatory PCDS, arguing buyers are better served by their own inspector. — [NYSBA summary](https://nysba.org/pcda-amended-500-seller-credit-deleted-and-additional-questions-added-to-pcds/)

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] What happens when a seller declines to fix: (1) the seller must answer the PCDS "Yes — known defect" for each item (or attach the report), on pain of §465(2) damages and fraud exposure; (2) the brokerage, if it has seen the report, must decide whether to disclose in listing remarks or to buyers (the §443 "should disclose" standard and §441-c), and cannot ethically run a "pre-inspected" marketing line while knowing of undisclosed defects; (3) price, credits or an "as is" contract clause are then negotiation tools, not substitutes for disclosure. Silence is not an option once the report exists — which is exactly the son-in-law's reservation stated as law.
- [INFERENCE] Deterrence is real and asymmetric: sellers with well-maintained houses gain little and risk little; sellers with deferred maintenance (the ones a "fix everything first" pitch targets) are the ones a cautious attorney will advise not to create knowledge. Expect sellers' attorneys — the people who used to recommend the $500 opt-out to avoid "catch-all" questions — to advise clients to decline the free inspection, or to accept it only with the report delivered to the seller alone and reviewed with counsel before the PCDS is completed.
- [INFERENCE] Programme mechanics that limit the damage: the seller (not the brokerage) is the client; the brokerage receives the report only with written seller consent and only after the seller's attorney has reviewed it against the PCDS; "repair-or-disclose" is spelled out in the listing agreement; listing remarks never characterise the inspection result; the seller's attorney completes the PCDS using the report; a revised PCDS is issued if the report post-dates an earlier one.

### Gaps

- No New York-specific survey of sellers' attorneys' attitudes to pre-listing inspections after March 2024.

## Key question 7 — Overall: pros, cons, liability matrix, and conditions under which it is worth doing

### Takeaway
As proposed — a friend upstate, paid by the brokerage, inspecting "everything" so buyers skip their own inspection — the offering is unlawful if the friend is unlicensed, structurally in tension with the inspector ethics rules if the brokerage is the payer or client, and strategically backwards in New York because it manufactures seller "actual knowledge" under a now-mandatory disclosure regime while buyers keep inspecting anyway. A narrower version can work: optional, seller-engaged, licensed and E&O-insured inspector at full retail price paid by the brokerage with written multi-party disclosure, report to the seller only, attorney review before the PCDS, and no buyer-facing "pre-inspected" claims.

### Inferences

**Pros (as supported by the evidence)**
- [INFERENCE] Early knowledge lets the seller repair at leisure, price accurately and avoid eleventh-hour renegotiation; NAR's November 2025 RCI (via HomeLight) attributes many delayed settlements to inspection issues, so fewer surprises plausibly means fewer delays — though no programme has published data proving it.
- [INFERENCE] A seller who repairs and then discloses honestly is in the strongest possible PCDA/fraud position (actual knowledge fully disclosed; "as is" plus specific disclaimers then bar most fraud claims — Masters; Serba).
- [INFERENCE] Differentiation for a software-driven brokerage: a structured, disclosed condition file could become the brokerage's data asset for pricing and for pre-filling the PCDS with the seller's attorney.

**Cons (as supported by the evidence)**
- [INFERENCE] The core premise — buyers skip their own inspection — is unsupported; ~80% inspect anyway (anecdotal), FHA tells buyers to, and New York buyers' counsel are told that not inspecting loses cases.
- [INFERENCE] The programme manufactures seller knowledge in a state whose disclosure safe harbour is "only actual knowledge"; the sellers most likely to want "fix everything first" are the ones whose attorneys will tell them not to create that knowledge.
- [INFERENCE] "Comprehensive" is not purchasable as one licensed service; radon, pests, septic, well and mold are separate scopes, each outside the licensed inspector's duty and standards.
- [INFERENCE] Hard cost of $900–1,800+ per listing, payable regardless of closing, with the brokerage barred from taking any inspector discount tied to referrals.
- [INFERENCE] The friend: if unlicensed, criminal exposure for him and the brokerage; if licensed, the friendship is itself a disclosable "potential conflict" under §197-4.7(b), and he cannot do the repairs before closing.

**Conditions under which it is worth doing**
- [INFERENCE] Licensed, E&O-insured NY home inspector (verify licence number; obtain certificate of E&O, not just the statutory GL), under a written pre-inspection agreement with the seller as client, with the §197-4.2 clauses, a conspicuous fee-cap clause, and written disclosure (signed by seller and brokerage) that the brokerage pays a fixed, non-contingent retail fee and that the inspector and brokerage principals are personal acquaintances.
- [INFERENCE] Report delivered to the seller only (five business days, §444-g(3)); the brokerage receives it only on the seller's written consent and only after the seller's attorney has reviewed it against the PCDS; listing remarks never characterise condition.
- [INFERENCE] "Comprehensive" re-scoped honestly: the licensed inspection plus separately contracted radon, water-quality, septic and WDI services where relevant, each priced and disclosed; test reports attached to the PCDS where the form requires it.
- [INFERENCE] Repair-or-disclose written into the listing agreement; the programme is optional and offered after the seller's attorney has explained the knowledge consequence; attorney (the attorney-broker, or independent counsel if conflicts rules require) completes the PCDS from the report.
- [INFERENCE] No buyer-facing claim that the buyer need not inspect; buyers are handed the PCDS language that the statement "is not a substitute for any home, pest, radon or other inspections" and encouraged to inspect — which also keeps the inspector and brokerage outside near-privity.
- [INFERENCE] Economic test: price the programme as a seller-retention/pricing-accuracy cost ($900–1,800+ per listing), not as a buyer-side saving; pilot on a small number of listings and measure renegotiation and fall-through rates before scaling, because no external data exists.

- No empirical evidence (any state) that pre-listing programmes shorten days on market or reduce renegotiations.

- Whether the attorney-broker's own professional-responsibility rules affect completing the PCDS for a seller-client of his brokerage was outside scope and not researched.
